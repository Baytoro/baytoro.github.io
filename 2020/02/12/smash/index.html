SMASH: One-Shot Model Architecture Search through HyperNetworks

H.J. Chen
  2020-02-12 2020-02-12 Created  
  • Work hard 
  • >
  • PaperReading 
 
  • NAS 

Brock A, Lim T, Ritchie J M, Weston N. SMASH: One-Shot Model Architecture Search through HyperNetworks. ICLR 2018.

Paper

SMASH is an early one-shot NAS method. Instead of training every candidate architecture from scratch, it trains a HyperNetwork that maps an architecture encoding to the weights of that architecture. Candidate models can then be evaluated with generated weights, which gives a much cheaper ranking signal.

This is a different path from DARTS-style weight sharing. DARTS keeps a shared supernet and selects subgraphs. SMASH explicitly generates weights conditioned on an architecture description.

The target is not perfect final accuracy. The target is ranking fidelity: architectures that look good under generated weights should also tend to be good after final training.

The figure shows the memory-bank view used to encode variable network configurations. SMASH has to represent architectures in a way that the HyperNetwork can consume and turn into convolution weights.

Training Signal

The HyperNetwork is trained by sampling architectures and updating (\phi) so that the generated weights perform well. This means the HyperNetwork learns a distribution over architecture-to-weight mappings, not a separate optimizer for every architecture.

There are two important consequences:

  • the architecture encoding must preserve enough structural information;
  • the HyperNetwork capacity limits the quality of the generated weights.

If the generator is too weak, it may rank candidates by how easy they are to generate rather than by how good they are after real training.

Why It Mattered

SMASH moved NAS cost from candidate training into a reusable evaluator. That idea later appears in several forms:

Later direction Related idea
one-shot supernets amortize architecture evaluation
predictor-based NAS learn a cheap ranking model
weight-sharing NAS avoid training each child from scratch

SMASH is not the same as these methods, but it helped establish the idea that architecture evaluation can be amortized.

Limitations

The generated weights are only a proxy. A model that performs well with HyperNetwork weights may not perform equally well after full training. This gap is the central risk.

The method also becomes harder as the search space grows. Generating weights for many convolutional shapes and connectivity patterns is not cheap, and the architecture encoding may fail to capture subtle graph differences.

Takeaway

SMASH is more historically important than directly practical today. It asked a useful question: can we train one model that lets us cheaply evaluate many architectures? That question sits behind much of modern one-shot and predictor-based NAS.
