MONAS: Multi-Objective Neural Architecture Search

H.J. Chen
  2020-01-16 2020-01-16 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 

Paper

MONAS is not trying to invent a new cell search space. The paper is more about the objective: if a model is going to run under a power or energy budget, searching only for validation accuracy is the wrong optimization problem.

The useful idea is straightforward: keep the usual controller-and-child-network NAS loop, but make the reward multi-objective. Accuracy is still there, but energy, peak power, or MAC operations can become part of the reward or a hard constraint.

Motivation

Early NAS papers mostly asked one question: which architecture gives the best validation accuracy? That is fine for benchmark chasing, but less useful for deployment. A model with slightly better accuracy can be a bad choice if it burns too much energy or violates a device power budget.

MONAS puts this deployment concern into the search loop instead of checking it after the fact. The paper evaluates this on CIFAR-10 using AlexNet-like and CondenseNet-like search spaces, then extends the idea with MONAS-S for a larger ENAS-style weight-sharing search space.

Contribution

The paper makes three concrete contributions:

  1. It formulates NAS as a multi-objective search problem, with reward functions that include accuracy, energy, peak power, or MAC constraints.
  2. It measures power and energy with NVIDIA nvprof, so the non-accuracy objective is tied to actual execution rather than only FLOPs.
  3. It extends the idea to MONAS-S, a weight-sharing version that searches a much larger space faster and biases the controller toward lower-MAC operations.

The paper’s most interesting claim is not that MONAS beats every classifier. It is that the controller can be steered toward a region of interest: under 70 W peak power, above 0.85 accuracy, or below a MAC threshold.

Methodology

Controller loop

MONAS keeps the classic NAS structure: an RNN controller samples a target CNN, the target CNN is trained/evaluated, and the measured objectives become the reward.

For the AlexNet-like search space, the controller predicts filter number, height, and width for each convolutional layer. For the CondenseNet-like search space, it predicts dense-block stage and growth values. This is a narrower space than later NAS cell-search papers, but it is enough to study objective design.

MONAS-S

MONAS-S borrows the weight-sharing idea from ENAS. Instead of training every sampled architecture from scratch, it trains a shared DAG and updates the controller from sampled subgraphs. This makes it practical to search a 12-layer space with roughly possible networks.

The target network contains 12 layers. The first 4 layers have the largest feature maps, the next 4 are half-size, and the last 4 are half-size again. This detail matters because early-layer operations dominate MAC cost.

Results

Constraint steering

The scatter plots show a useful sanity check. Random search spreads samples across the space. MONAS concentrates samples near the requested region:

Constraint Satisfied samples
Peak power < 70 W 340 / 600
Accuracy >= 0.85 498 / 600

This is the part I like: the controller is not just finding a high-accuracy model; it is learning where to spend search budget under a user-defined constraint.

CondenseNet comparison

MONAS also finds CondenseNet-like configurations with better energy-accuracy tradeoffs than hand-picked baselines.

Model Error Energy / 1000 inf.
CondenseNet 122 4.48 129.37 J
CondenseNet 110 4.63 108.74 J
CondenseNet 74 5.28 56.35 J
CondenseNet-MONAS 4.34 92.16 J
CondenseNet-MONAS 5.07 42.46 J
CondenseNet-MONAS 5.60 34.88 J

This table is a reminder that “best” depends on where you are on the Pareto front. A 4.34% model is accurate but not the cheapest; a 5.60% model is much cheaper but gives up accuracy.

MAC-aware search with MONAS-S

When MONAS-S adds a MAC constraint, the sampled architectures move toward the lower-MAC region. The paper compares this against ENAS-like sampling without the MAC constraint, both trained for 600 epochs and then sampled for 1000 target networks.

The operation distribution explains where the saving comes from. MONAS-S uses more MAC-efficient operations, especially in early layers where feature maps are large. That is exactly the right place to reduce computation.

Takeaway

MONAS is an early reminder that NAS should optimize the real objective, not just the leaderboard metric. Accuracy-only NAS can easily produce architectures that are awkward for deployment.

The limitation is that the paper’s search spaces are still relatively constrained, and the hardware measurements are tied to the tested GPU setup. But the main lesson transfers well: make the search reward reflect the target constraint, then look at the Pareto front instead of a single scalar “best model”.
