Large-Scale Evolution of Image Classifiers

H.J. Chen
  2020-01-18 2020-01-18 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 

Real E, Moore S, Selle A, et al. Large-Scale Evolution of Image Classifiers. ICML 2017.

Paper

This paper is an early large-scale neural architecture search result, but it does not look like later RL-controller NAS or differentiable NAS. The algorithm keeps a population of trained CNNs, repeatedly mutates selected parents, trains the children, and uses validation accuracy as the selection signal.

The interesting part is how little algorithmic machinery is needed. The search is black-box, asynchronous, and almost embarrassingly parallel. The expensive part is that every child still has to be trained for real.

Result Positioning

The paper reports 94.6% CIFAR-10 accuracy for a single evolved model, 95.6% for an ensemble, and 77.0% on CIFAR-100. At the time, this was not just a toy architecture search demo.

Table 1 compares the evolved model with hand-designed CNNs. The evolved CIFAR-10 model is below the strongest Wide ResNet and DenseNet numbers, but it is competitive with a much less human-designed starting point. The “Reachable?” column also matters: some hand-designed baselines live outside the search space.

Table 2 is the more relevant comparison. Against other automatically discovered architectures, the evolutionary run reaches 94.6% on CIFAR-10, 77.0% on CIFAR-100, and 95.6% with an ensemble. The starting point is deliberately weak: a single layer with zero convolutions.

Search Loop

The evolutionary loop is pairwise tournament selection:

Fitness is validation accuracy. The population size in the main experiments is 1000, and the paper reports running experiments over 250 parallel workers. Each child is trained for 25,600 SGD steps with momentum 0.9, batch size 50, and weight decay 0.0001.

The search object is a full CNN graph plus training hyperparameters, not a neat repeated cell. Mutations can insert or remove convolutions, alter filter size, channel count, stride, learning rate, skip connections, and weight state. Some mutations preserve all weights, some preserve none, and many preserve only the compatible subset.

What Evolution Finds

Figure 1 shows a useful shape: many weak individuals appear early, then the population quickly moves into a high-accuracy band. The architecture sketches attached to lineage points are also instructive. The evolved networks are not clean human-designed templates; they contain stacked convolutions, skips, and repeated convolution-BN-ReLU patterns in places that a human probably would not draw first.

This is why I would not summarize the paper as “evolution invents ResNet.” It is closer to: given a mutation grammar and enough training budget, local search can build competent CNN programs from very weak initial conditions.

Controls

The controls are important. Random search is much worse in the reported wall-clock comparison. Evolution without weight inheritance is close early on but stays lower later. That does not prove evolutionary search is always better, but it does show the result is not only coming from repeatedly sampling random architectures.

The paper also makes a practical point about asynchronous search. Fast-training bad models can return early, enter the population, and get more chances to reproduce. That implicit bias is part of the system, so any reproduction should preserve the worker behavior instead of only copying the mutation list.

Meta-Parameters

The population-size and training-step sweeps explain why small NAS demos can be misleading. Larger populations explore more lineages, and longer child training gives the fitness estimate a better chance to reflect the architecture rather than just early optimization noise.

The downside is direct: the main result costs a huge amount of compute. The paper reports the best CIFAR-10 run at about 4 x 10^20 FLOPs, with the five-experiment average around 9 x 10^19 FLOPs. This is not a cheap recipe.

Local Optima

Figure 4 is a good reminder that evolutionary search is not magic. Populations can plateau. The authors tried increasing the mutation rate and resetting weights to help populations escape local optima. Both can work, but both are blunt tools.

The weight reset example is especially interesting. Weight inheritance helps make full-model evolution practical, but it can also make a mediocre architecture look good because it has accumulated more training. Resetting weights partially breaks that advantage and forces the population to re-evaluate architecture quality.

Takeaway

Large-Scale Evolution is rough by modern NAS benchmark standards. The search space is broad, the compute budget is large, and the results are not directly comparable to later cell-based search spaces or tabular NAS benchmarks.

The durable lesson is simpler: architecture search does not need a controller, relaxation, or differentiable proxy to be useful. If the evaluation loop is scalable and the mutation operators preserve enough structure, plain evolutionary pressure can discover strong trained models.
