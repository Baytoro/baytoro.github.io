SNAS: Stochastic Neural Architecture Search

H.J. Chen
  2020-08-28 2020-08-28 Created  
  • Work hard 
  • >
  • PaperReading 
 
  • NAS 

Xie S, Zheng H, Liu C, Lin L. SNAS: Stochastic Neural Architecture Search. ICLR 2019.

  • Paper: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09926.pdf
  • Code: https://github.com/SNAS-Series/SNAS-Series

SNAS is a useful paper to read right after DARTS and ENAS. It keeps the familiar cell-based NAS search space, but changes the variable being optimized. Instead of training a deterministic soft mixture of operations like DARTS, SNAS samples architecture decisions from a learned distribution and makes that sampling differentiable with a Concrete, or Gumbel-Softmax, relaxation.

The paper’s claim is not just “use Gumbel noise”. The claim is that NAS can be written as optimizing the expected loss of sampled child graphs, while still allowing gradients to flow through both operation weights and architecture distribution parameters in the same backpropagation pass. That makes SNAS sit between three families:

  • RL NAS samples discrete architectures but uses delayed rewards.
  • DARTS uses direct gradients but trains a deterministic supernet mixture.
  • SNAS samples stochastic child graphs and uses a differentiable relaxation to update the sampling distribution.

The practical result is a search method that reports competitive CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-transfer results with about 1.5 GPU days of search, while preserving the search-time validation accuracy much better than DARTS in the paper’s own comparison.

Credit Assignment

The paper connects SNAS to policy-gradient NAS, but with a more local signal. In RL NAS, the controller receives a reward after a sampled architecture has been trained or validated. That reward is delayed and coarse. SNAS uses the differentiable training loss itself, so the architecture parameter receives gradients through the actual computational graph.

A high-level policy-gradient view has the familiar score-function shape:

but the Concrete reparameterization gives a pathwise gradient rather than relying only on a scalar terminal reward. The paper interprets this as more efficient credit assignment: operation and topology decisions receive feedback from how they affect the differentiable loss, not only from a final architecture-level score.

This is also where SNAS differs from ENAS. ENAS shares weights and samples architectures, but its controller is still a sequential decision process trained by reinforcement learning. SNAS removes the controller MDP assumption and directly parameterizes a factorizable architecture distribution over cell edges.

Search Behavior

The search-stage comparison is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the paper. SNAS is compared with DARTS and ENAS across randomly generated seeds.

The paper’s interpretation is:

  • DARTS reaches high search validation accuracy quickly, but the final derived child graph can be very different from the relaxed parent network.
  • ENAS improves more slowly and plateaus lower in this plot.
  • SNAS keeps a stochastic child-graph objective, so its search validation accuracy is more meaningful after derivation.

The numbers are stark:

Method Search validation accuracy Child-network validation accuracy Search cost
DARTS, second-order bilevel 87.67 54.66 1 GPU day
SNAS, single-level, mild constraint 88.54 90.67 1.5 GPU days

This does not prove every DARTS run collapses this badly. It does show the exact failure mode SNAS is designed around: a relaxed supernet can look good before discretization and then lose quality when converted to a child graph. SNAS tries to make the search-time model closer to the thing that will be evaluated.

The moderate constraint row is the best SNAS row in the table: 2.85 +/- 0.02% test error with 2.8M parameters. It does not beat second-order DARTS on error, but it uses fewer parameters and is much better than single-level DARTS. That is the fair comparison for the paper’s optimization claim, because SNAS is also using a single-level setup.

The aggressive constraint row is also worth keeping. It gives up accuracy, moving to 3.10 +/- 0.04%, but shrinks the searched model to 2.3M parameters. That supports the idea that the resource constraint is a real knob, not only a regularizer.

ImageNet Transfer

SNAS follows the usual NASNet/DARTS protocol: search on CIFAR-10, then transfer the discovered cell to ImageNet mobile setting.

The SNAS row reports:

Method Top-1 error Top-5 error Params MAdds Search cost
DARTS 26.9 9.0 4.9M 595M 1 GPU day
SNAS, mild constraint 27.3 9.2 4.3M 522M 1.5 GPU days

This is a reasonable transfer result, not a dominant one. DARTS is slightly better on top-1 and top-5 error. SNAS is smaller and uses fewer multiply-adds. The useful takeaway is that the stochastic cells are not CIFAR-only artifacts; they transfer about as well as the DARTS-family cells in this mobile ImageNet setting.

Relation To DARTS, ENAS, And GDAS

SNAS is easiest to place by comparing the training graph each method uses.

  • ENAS samples discrete child graphs from a controller and trains them with shared weights. Its weak point is delayed RL reward and controller training.
  • DARTS trains a deterministic soft mixture of all candidate operations. Its weak point is the mismatch between the relaxed supernet and the derived child graph.
  • SNAS samples relaxed edge masks from a Concrete distribution. Its weak point is that temperature scheduling and final discretization still matter.
  • GDAS samples one operation per edge in the forward pass. Its weak point is still the final discretization rule and the hand-designed search space.

SNAS and GDAS are especially close in spirit. Both use differentiable stochastic sampling to reduce the DARTS relaxation gap. The emphasis is different:

  • SNAS is framed around optimizing a distribution over child graphs and connecting search gradients to policy gradients.
  • GDAS is framed around sampling one differentiable subgraph per step to reduce memory and make search computation closer to the final architecture.

So I would not read SNAS as “the final answer” to differentiable NAS. I would read it as one of the papers that makes the DARTS relaxation less naive.

Limitations

Several caveats matter when using SNAS as a baseline.

  • The search space is still hand-designed. SNAS searches inside a NASNet/DARTS-style cell grammar.
  • The fully factorizable distribution ignores correlations between edge decisions. That makes optimization simpler, but it is not a universal architecture prior.
  • The Concrete temperature schedule can change the result. A bad schedule either makes the model too soft or collapses the distribution too early.
  • The reported ImageNet result is transfer from CIFAR-10, not direct ImageNet search.
  • Search cost comparisons are hardware- and implementation-dependent. The paper uses NVIDIA TITAN Xp GPUs for the main search setting.
  • The final architecture is still derived by turning a learned stochastic distribution into a discrete cell, so the relaxation gap is reduced but not eliminated.

Takeaway

SNAS matters because it separates two ideas that are often conflated in differentiable NAS. Gradients do not require a deterministic soft mixture over all operations. You can keep architecture choices as stochastic variables, relax the samples for backpropagation, and optimize the expected loss of child graphs.

My main practical lesson from the paper is about objective alignment. If the final model will be a discrete child graph, the search-time computation should look as much like a child graph as possible. SNAS pushes in that direction with Concrete-relaxed sampling and a decomposable resource penalty. Later one-path and hardware-aware NAS methods keep returning to the same theme: the supernet is only useful if its training signal predicts the quality and cost of the model you will actually deploy.
