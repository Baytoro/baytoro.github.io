Progressive Neural Architecture Search

H.J. Chen
  2020-04-19 2020-04-19 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 

Liu C, Zoph B, Shlens J, et al. Progressive Neural Architecture Search. ECCV 2018.

Paper

PNAS is a practical answer to the main weakness of early NAS papers: the controller or evolution algorithm spends a huge amount of compute on complete cells before it has any useful knowledge about the space. The paper keeps the NASNet-style cell idea, but changes the search procedure. Instead of sampling a full 5-block cell directly, it grows cells from 1 block to 5 blocks. At each depth, a predictor ranks the expanded candidates, and only the top beam is actually trained.

The key point is that PNAS is not differentiable NAS. It is closer to sequential model-based optimization: evaluate a small batch of candidates, fit a surrogate, use the surrogate to prune the next expansion, then spend real training only on the survivors.

Why This Paper Matters

Before PNAS, the typical NAS story was expensive. NASNet uses an RNN controller trained by reinforcement learning, then performs an additional re-ranking stage. AmoebaNet improves the final model but still pays a large search budget. PNAS asks a narrower question: if the architecture is a graph built from small blocks, can we search the graph in an order that avoids most of the combinatorial explosion?

The answer is yes, as long as three assumptions are good enough:

  • small cells contain signal about larger cells;
  • the predictor ranks candidates well enough, especially near the top;
  • the beam size is large enough to avoid pruning every promising partial design.

This is why I read PNAS as a sample-efficiency paper, not mainly as a new architecture paper. The final PNASNet cell is useful, but the more reusable idea is the search schedule.

Cell Search Space

PNAS searches for a convolutional cell. A full CNN is built by stacking this cell many times. The paper deliberately learns only one cell type. A cell with stride 1 behaves like a normal cell, and a cell with stride 2 behaves like a reduction cell. This makes the search space smaller than the original NASNet space, which searches normal and reduction cells separately.

Predictor Design

The predictor is the fragile part of the method. If it ranks useful children too low, they disappear from the search tree permanently. The paper therefore evaluates the predictor with rank correlation, not just regression error.

There are two predictors:

  • an RNN/LSTM predictor that reads the cell as a variable-length token sequence;
  • an MLP predictor that embeds each block and averages block embeddings into a fixed-length cell representation.

CIFAR-10 Results

The headline CIFAR-10 result is PNASNet-5 with 3.41 +/- 0.09 percent error, 3.2M parameters, and 1.0B search examples. NASNet-A has the same 3.41 percent error with 3.3M parameters, but its reported cost is 21.4B to 29.3B examples. AmoebaNet-A/B reach lower errors, but they pay substantially higher search cost.

I would summarize Table 3 this way:

PNASNet-5 3.41 +/- 0.09 3.2M 1.0B

PNAS is not the absolute best accuracy row. Its selling point is the Pareto position: NASNet-level accuracy with a much smaller search budget.

ImageNet Transfer

The ImageNet transfer results are also competitive. In the mobile setting, PNASNet-5 uses 5.1M parameters and 588M multiply-adds, reaching 74.2 top-1 and 91.9 top-5. In the large setting, it uses 86.1M parameters and 25.0B multiply-adds, reaching 82.9 top-1 and 96.2 top-5.

The important observation is not just the final ImageNet accuracy. The cell was searched on CIFAR-10, then scaled. This makes the search much cheaper, but it relies on CIFAR-10 being a useful proxy for ImageNet.

The appendix checks this transfer assumption directly.

The rank correlation between CIFAR-10 and ImageNet accuracy is 0.727 for top-1 and 0.713 for top-5 over the tested architectures. That supports CIFAR-10 as a coarse proxy. The paper also notes that the correlation among high-CIFAR models is weaker: 0.505 for top-1 and 0.460 for top-5 when CIFAR accuracy is above 0.89. This is the expected regime where proxy search becomes noisy, because most candidates are already strong.

My Reading Notes

The strongest part of PNAS is the way it turns an architecture into a growing object. A full cell is not sampled as a single opaque token. It is assembled in a curriculum from simple to complex graphs. That makes the search much more sample-efficient than a controller that starts by sampling full cells.

The weakest part is also clear: pruning is irreversible. If the predictor assigns a low score to a partial cell, all descendants of that cell disappear. The paper reduces this risk with a fairly large beam, predictor ensembling, and repeated real evaluations at every depth, but the method still depends on a useful correlation between partial-cell promise and final-cell quality.

This also explains why rank correlation is the right diagnostic. For search, calibrated accuracy prediction is less important than ordering. A predictor can have mediocre absolute error and still be useful if the top of its ranking is good enough.

Takeaway

PNAS is a pragmatic NAS paper. It keeps the cell-based search space from NASNet, but changes the expensive part: instead of evaluating many complete cells, it progressively grows cells and uses a learned predictor to decide which children deserve training. The result is not magic, but the cost comparison is convincing: about the same CIFAR-10 error as NASNet-A with roughly 1.0B search examples instead of more than 21B.

The broader lesson still applies outside classic NAS: when the design space is compositional, do not score every full design from scratch. Grow partial designs, learn a cheap ranking model, and spend the expensive evaluation budget only where the ranking says it matters.
