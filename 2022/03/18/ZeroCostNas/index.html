Zero-Cost Proxies for Lightweight NAS

H.J. Chen
  2022-03-18 2022-03-18 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 

Reference

  • Paper: Zero-Cost Proxies for Lightweight NAS
  • Authors: Mohamed S. Abdelfattah, Abhinav Mehrotra, Lukasz Dudziak, Nicholas D. Lane
  • Venue: ICLR 2021

This paper asks a practical NAS question: before training an architecture, can we compute a cheap signal at initialization that is correlated enough with its final accuracy?

The answer is not “training is unnecessary.” The more useful answer is: a zero-cost score can be good enough to warm up a search algorithm, guide local mutations, or filter many bad candidates before spending real training budget.

The name “zero-cost” is a little aggressive. These proxies still need model construction, initialization, sometimes a minibatch, and often a forward/backward pass. The cost is close to zero compared with training hundreds or thousands of candidate networks.

One Sentence

Zero-cost NAS turns pruning-at-initialization signals such as snip, grasp, synflow, fisher, and jacob_cov into architecture-level scores by aggregating saliency over the whole network, then uses those scores to rank architectures without training them.

My short reading: the paper is valuable because it treats zero-cost proxies as search tools, not as magic accuracy predictors. The best use case is not selecting one final model from a raw score. The best use case is spending a very cheap pass to bias a downstream NAS algorithm toward better regions of the search space.

Why This Problem Matters

Classical NAS spends most of its budget evaluating architectures. A candidate can be evaluated by full training, early stopping, reduced input resolution, fewer channels, fewer samples, weight sharing, or a learned surrogate. All of those proxies still have visible cost or bias.

The paper uses EcoNAS as the conventional reduced-training baseline. EcoNAS reduces the training setting along axes such as resolution, channel width, dataset size, and epoch count. That is reasonable, but it still runs training loops. On a real GPU, the wall-clock time of small reduced models can also be much worse than their FLOP count suggests.

EcoNAS-style reduced training can produce useful rank correlation, but its theoretical FLOP reduction does not fully translate into wall-clock speedup.

Figure 1 is a good warning. On NAS-Bench-201 CIFAR-10, the original EcoNAS-like setting r8 c4 e20 reaches only about rho = 0.61 when evaluated over all 15,625 architectures. The stronger econas+ configuration improves the rank correlation, but it needs more computation. The right plot is especially important: a point with FLOPs around one tenth of a full training epoch can still take time comparable to several full epochs because tiny models do not saturate the GPU well.

This is where zero-cost scores become attractive. They replace reduced training with a minibatch-level diagnostic at random initialization.

Turning Pruning Scores Into NAS Scores

Many zero-cost metrics start as pruning-at-initialization criteria. In pruning, the question is:

  • Which parameters or channels are least important and can be removed?

In NAS, the question becomes:

The intuition is that a trainable network should produce useful gradients at initialization. If gradients are tiny, broken, or concentrated in a pathological way, the model may train poorly. But this metric is blunt. A large gradient norm can also mean unstable scale, more parameters, or bad normalization. So I read grad_norm as a cheap trainability diagnostic, not as a precise predictor.

In NAS, that same property is useful because many search spaces contain skip, zero, or weak operations that can break signal flow.

I would not interpret SynFlow as “accuracy without data.” It is closer to “does this randomly initialized architecture have healthy signal paths?” The paper’s empirical result is that this path signal is surprisingly correlated with final performance in several NAS spaces.

Lower correlation means the randomly initialized architecture is already more input-sensitive and potentially more expressive.

This metric is more search-space dependent than it first appears. It works well inside NAS-Bench-201, but the paper shows it can fail when comparing unrelated PyTorchCV models or NAS-Bench-ASR models.

The vote score is not a new theory; it is a practical way to reduce single-proxy brittleness.

NAS-Bench-201: The Cleanest Test

NAS-Bench-201 has 15,625 architectures in a compact cell search space. Each directed edge chooses one operation from zero, skip connection, 1x1 convolution, 3x3 convolution, or 3x3 average pooling. Because every architecture has tabulated training results on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet16-120, it is a natural place to test rank correlation.

Zero-cost proxy points are plotted against the cost and rank correlation of early validation accuracy and EcoNAS-style reduced training.

Figure 2 is the main punchline. On NAS-Bench-201, zero-cost proxies sit far to the left of reduced-training curves because they need only minibatch-level computation. Yet synflow, jacob_cov, and especially vote reach competitive or better rank correlation.

Table 1: Spearman rank correlation on NAS-Bench-201.

The table is worth reading carefully:

  • grad_norm and snip are around 0.58 to 0.64. They are useful, but not strong enough to trust alone.
  • grasp is weaker on CIFAR-10 but becomes closer on CIFAR-100 and ImageNet16-120.
  • fisher is poor here, around 0.33 to 0.39.
  • synflow is consistently strong: 0.74, 0.76, and 0.75.
  • jacob_cov is also strong: 0.73, 0.71, and 0.71.
  • vote is best: 0.82, 0.83, and 0.82.

The cost comparison is the real result. The paper notes that the vote-style proxy costs about three minibatches, while econas+ is closer to a thousand minibatches. On this benchmark, a few initialization-time signals can beat a reduced-training proxy in rank correlation.

Beyond NAS-Bench-201

A proxy that works only on NAS-Bench-201 is not enough. NAS-Bench-201 is small, cell-based, and tightly controlled. The paper therefore tests a broader PyTorchCV model zoo and other tabular NAS spaces.

PyTorchCV is a heterogeneous setting: models differ in family, depth, width, and macro design.

The PyTorchCV experiment is a good stress test because it compares unrelated model families such as ResNets, DenseNets, MobileNets, and EfficientNets. The ranking problem is less controlled than a cell search space.

Two observations matter:

  • synflow, snip, fisher, and grad_norm remain usable across several datasets, with SynFlow especially strong on SVHN.
  • grasp and jacob_cov become unstable in this heterogeneous setting, even though jacob_cov was strong inside NAS-Bench-201.

That suggests a useful rule: a proxy can be reliable inside a homogeneous search space but fail when architecture families change too much.

Table 2: correlations on NAS-Bench-101, NAS-Bench-NLP, and NAS-Bench-ASR.

Table 2 makes the same point more sharply:

  • On NAS-Bench-101, grasp, synflow, and jacob_cov are all modest, around 0.37 to 0.45.
  • On NAS-Bench-NLP, jacob_cov is best at 0.56, while synflow is 0.34.
  • On NAS-Bench-ASR, synflow is best among reported metrics at 0.40, while jacob_cov becomes negative.

So the paper’s claim is not “SynFlow always wins.” The stronger claim is: SynFlow is the most consistently useful proxy across the tested NAS benchmarks. It may not be the best in every single table, but it rarely collapses as badly as some alternatives.

The one caution is reinforcement learning with too much warmup. The paper notes that 15k warmup points can hurt RL because the controller starts overfitting to the SynFlow reward rather than learning the real objective.

NAS-Bench-ASR TIMIT: the same SynFlow idea also helps on a speech-recognition search space.

Table 8: NAS-Bench-ASR search summary.

The ASR result is important because it is not just another image-classification cell benchmark. The target metric is phoneme error rate, and lower is better. To reach PER = 21.3%, the paper reports:

  • baseline random search and RL require more than 1000 trained models;
  • baseline aging evolution needs 138 trained models;
  • with 2000 SynFlow warmup points, those counts become 68, 173, and 87.

That is exactly how I would want a zero-cost proxy to behave: not perfect ranking, but a large reduction in the number of expensive evaluations.

NAS-Bench-101 is larger and has weaker SynFlow correlation than NAS-Bench-201. Even there, the paper reports strong sample-efficiency gains. The random-search-with-warmup result reaches 94.22% test accuracy after training only 34 models, compared with prior results using 140 to 256 trained models.

Why SynFlow Helps

The discussion section is useful because it does not stop at global Spearman correlation. It asks whether SynFlow puts good architectures near the top and whether it chooses good local moves.

Table 4: how many top-5% architectures appear inside the top 64 models ranked by SynFlow.

Randomly selecting 64 models would return about 5% top-5% models, roughly 3 models. SynFlow returns much more:

  • 44, 54, and 56 on the three NAS-Bench-201 datasets;
  • 12 on NAS-Bench-101;
  • 16 on NAS-Bench-ASR.

This explains why warmup works. An initial evolution pool or predictor warmup set becomes much richer in good candidates.

Table 5: probability that SynFlow chooses the best model inside a local edit-distance-one cluster.

For move proposal, the relevant question is local. In NAS-Bench-201, a random mutation has about a 1/25, or 4%, chance of selecting the best neighbor. SynFlow raises this to roughly 32% to 35%. On NAS-Bench-101, it is about 12%, close to the random cluster-size baseline but still useful. On NAS-Bench-ASR, the gain is smaller, from about 7.7% random chance to 10%.

This makes the method feel less mysterious. SynFlow helps because it enriches the candidate set and makes local mutation less random.

Top-Region And Local Analysis

The appendix tables are worth including because they reveal where global correlation hides details.

Table 9: Spearman correlation restricted to the top 10% of points.

Top-10% correlation is much harder than global correlation. Some metrics that look reasonable globally become negative or near zero at the top. SynFlow is not perfect, but it stays nonnegative and useful across the reported spaces: 0.18, 0.42, 0.55, 0.14, 0.10, and 0.40.

Table 10: percentage of truly top-10% models that also appear in each proxy’s top-10%.

Table 10 uses overlap rather than rank correlation. This is often closer to what NAS needs. On NAS-Bench-201, SynFlow retrieves 46%, 50%, and 44% of the true top-10% models inside its top-10% set. It is also the strongest metric on NAS-Bench-101. But the ASR row is a reminder that proxy behavior is not universal: jacob_cov has high top-10% overlap there even though its global rank correlation is poor.

Table 12: local rank correlation inside edit-distance-one neighborhoods.

Local correlation can differ from global correlation. On NAS-Bench-201, SynFlow and jacob_cov are both strong locally. On NAS-Bench-ASR, grad_norm and fisher have better local correlation than SynFlow. This means the best proxy for global warmup may not be the best proxy for local mutation in every search space.

Table 13: how often each proxy picks the best model in a local cluster.

Table 13 is the most operational local metric. SynFlow picks the best local model about one third of the time on NAS-Bench-201, which is much better than random. On NAS-Bench-101 it is also the best among the listed metrics. On NAS-Bench-ASR, several metrics are close. Again, the practical lesson is to validate proxy choice on the target search space before relying on it.

Failure Modes

I would be careful with zero-cost NAS for several reasons.

First, many scores are scale-sensitive. If an architecture has more parameters, more active paths, or different normalization behavior, a raw sum of saliencies can become a model-size proxy rather than an accuracy proxy.

Second, minibatch-dependent scores depend on the minibatch. snip, grasp, grad_norm, fisher, and jacob_cov can change with batch size, labels, augmentation, BatchNorm mode, and random seed. That does not make them useless, but it means the scoring protocol must be fixed.

Third, search-space homogeneity matters. jacob_cov is strong inside NAS-Bench-201 but weak across heterogeneous PyTorchCV models. A score that compares similar cell variants may not compare ResNet-like, MobileNet-like, and EfficientNet-like models fairly.

Fourth, a proxy can be gamed by the search algorithm. The RL warmup failure with too many SynFlow points is a good example. The controller can optimize the proxy instead of the final objective.

Finally, global rank correlation is not enough. A proxy should be checked on top-region overlap and local mutation decisions. Those are closer to the decisions a NAS algorithm actually makes.

How I Would Use It

I would not use zero-cost scores to directly choose the final architecture. I would use them as a cheap prior.

My practical workflow would be:

  1. Sample a few hundred or a few thousand architectures from the target search space.
  2. Compute synflow, snip, grad_norm, and maybe jacob_cov under a fixed initialization and minibatch protocol.
  3. Train a small calibration subset to check direction, global Spearman correlation, top-k overlap, and local neighbor accuracy.
  4. Use the best proxy or a small vote ensemble for warmup.
  5. Keep final model selection based on real training or a stronger surrogate.

For a homogeneous cell space like NAS-Bench-201, SynFlow is a strong default. For a heterogeneous model zoo or a different task such as ASR, I would not assume the same proxy is best. I would spend a small budget to calibrate the proxy first.

Implementation details I would keep fixed:

  • initialization scheme;
  • train/eval mode for BatchNorm and dropout;
  • minibatch size and labels for data-dependent scores;
  • whether scores are normalized by parameter count, layer count, or operation type;
  • random seed repetitions;
  • score direction, because some metrics are better when lower and others when higher.

The last point matters. The paper’s tables report rank correlations after using each metric in its intended direction. In a new codebase, I would always verify whether larger score means better architecture before using it inside search.

Takeaway

Zero-cost NAS is not a replacement for training. It is a way to buy a surprisingly informative ranking signal before training.

The core insight is that an architecture’s initialization already exposes useful structure: gradient flow, path connectivity, activation saliency, and input-Jacobian diversity. Aggregated carefully, those signals can guide NAS algorithms with a tiny fraction of the evaluation cost.

For me, the strongest result is not just Table 1’s high vote correlation on NAS-Bench-201. It is the combination of Figure 4, Figure 5, and the local-analysis tables: even when the proxy is imperfect, it can still improve the actual search loop by seeding better candidates and making better local moves.
