Zen-NAS: A Zero-Shot NAS for High-Performance Deep Image Recognition

H.J. Chen
  2022-04-11 2022-04-11 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 
  • | Zero-Shot 

Reference

Lin M, Wang P, Sun Z, et al. Zen-NAS: A Zero-Shot NAS for High-Performance Deep Image Recognition. ICCV 2021.

  • Paper: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.01063.pdf
  • Code: https://github.com/idstcv/ZenNAS
  • Related notes: Zero-Cost NAS, TE-NAS

Zen-NAS is a zero-shot NAS paper for CNN image recognition. It does not train a supernet, does not train sampled candidates, and does not learn an accuracy predictor. It scores each architecture at random initialization with Zen-Score, then uses evolutionary search to keep architectures with high score under latency / FLOPs constraints.

The method is best read as a very practical proxy paper. The paper’s claim is not “initialization score fully explains generalization.” The useful claim is narrower: inside a constrained CNN macro search space, an expressivity score that is cheap, scale-corrected, and hardware-budget-aware can find strong ImageNet networks.

The headline plot is strong. On ImageNet with NVIDIA V100 FP16 latency, ZenNets sit on a better accuracy-latency frontier than common manual and NAS-designed CNN families. The largest ZenNet reports 83.6% top-1 and about 0.5 GPU day search cost.

What Zen-Score Measures

Zero-cost NAS proxies often ask whether a randomly initialized architecture looks trainable. Zen-NAS instead frames the score as expressivity. The paper starts from a ReLU CNN as a piecewise-linear function. For a vanilla CNN , the paper defines a -score:

This is different from SNIP / GradNorm-style proxies, which differentiate the loss with respect to weights. Zen-NAS differentiates with respect to input, and it is not label-dependent.

Why Plain Phi-Score Breaks

The score above is not immediately usable. If BatchNorm is removed, deep networks can overflow because feature magnitude grows with depth. If BatchNorm is kept, the raw score becomes rescaled by BN statistics and can flatten architecture differences.

Figure 2 is the key diagnostic:

  • without BN, -score grows with depth and overflows around 30 layers;
  • with BN, overflow disappears, but the score is scaled down;
  • for bottleneck-width changes, the BN version can become almost constant;
  • Zen-Score restores a monotonic signal after compensating for BN scale.

This is the part that makes the paper more than “use gradient norm as a proxy.” The paper is explicitly debugging why a naive expressivity proxy becomes numerically useless in modern CNNs.

  • the score is computed on the pre-GAP feature map, not final logits.

This graph explains the BN correction. The score measures how much the feature map changes when the input mini-batch is perturbed, then multiplies in the per-BN channel deviation terms. The theorem in the paper argues that this recovers the scale of the corresponding no-BN vanilla network up to an approximation error.

Search Procedure

Zen-NAS uses evolutionary search as a simple architecture generator. The generator is not the main contribution; the score is. Still, the constraints are important:

  1. Start from an initial structure.
  2. Mutate block type, kernel size, width, and depth.
  3. Reject candidates that violate latency / FLOPs budget or maximum depth.
  4. Compute Zen-Score for feasible candidates.
  5. Keep the population by removing the lowest-score network.
  6. Return the highest-score network after fixed iterations.

The search space is not arbitrary graph search. The paper uses CNN blocks inspired by ResNet / MobileNet families, with hardware or FLOPs constraints. This matters because a zero-shot score is only comparable when the architectures share enough grammar. I would not assume the same score ranks arbitrary DAG cells, Transformer blocks, or heavily skip-connected spaces without recalibration.

Proxy Comparison

The paper compares Zen-Score with FLOPs, gradient norm, SynFlow, TE-Score, NASWOT, and random search on CIFAR under the same architecture search loop.

The compact view:

Proxy C10 C100
Zen-Score 96.2 80.1
TE-Score 96.1 77.2
NASWOT 96.0 77.5
SynFlow 95.1 75.9
FLOPs 93.1 64.7
Random 93.5 71.1

The CIFAR-10 difference is small among stronger proxies. CIFAR-100 is more revealing: Zen-Score is several points ahead of TE-Score and NASWOT in this experiment. FLOPs and gradient norm are bad proxies here, even worse than random search on CIFAR-100.

On 224x224 ResNet-18 / ResNet-50, Zen-Score is reported as roughly 20-28x faster than TE-Score. NASWOT is closer but still slower in these rows, and the authors note an Inf-score issue for the official NASWOT implementation on ResNet-18/50.

ImageNet Results

The ImageNet experiments use two target constraints:

  • latency on NVIDIA V100 FP16, batch size 64;
  • FLOPs for lightweight models, with SE blocks added.

The V100 latency result was the first figure. The paper reports that ZenNet-1.2ms reaches 83.6% top-1, between EfficientNet-B5 and B6, while being about 4.9x faster at the same accuracy level.

The FLOPs plot tells a similar story. ZenNet-900M-SE reaches 80.8% top-1, which the paper compares to EfficientNet-B3 with about 43-44% fewer FLOPs. OFANet is the closest competitor in this plot.

Search Cost

Table 3 is the reason Zen-NAS is interesting as a systems paper:

Method Top-1 Search cost
TE-NAS 74.1 0.2 GPU day
PC-DARTS 75.8 3.8 GPU days
OFANet 80.1 51.6 GPU days
EfficientNet-B7 84.4 3800 GPU days
Zen-NAS 83.6 0.5 GPU day

The comparison is not perfectly fair because NAS papers use different search spaces, training recipes, hardware, and accounting rules. The paper says this explicitly. Still, the contrast is useful: Zen-NAS is not merely saving a small constant factor. It tries to move ImageNet NAS from trained evaluation to initialization-time scoring.

The paper estimates that scoring 96,000 ResNet-50-like networks takes about 4 GPU hours, or 0.17 GPU day; the full reported search cost is 0.5 GPU day after including the broader setup.

How I Read It

Zen-NAS is strongest when read together with its assumptions:

  • The score is an expressivity proxy, not a generalization theorem.
  • The BN correction is essential; otherwise the proxy can overflow or lose ranking power.
  • The search space is a CNN macro space with controlled block mutations.
  • The final architectures still need full training for the reported accuracies.
  • Hardware constraints are part of the search, not an afterthought.

The method also explains why “zero-cost NAS” is not one thing. NASWOT, SynFlow, TE-NAS, and Zen-NAS measure different properties at initialization: activation patterns, path saliency, NTK / linear regions, and input-feature sensitivity. A proxy can be cheap and still fail if it measures the wrong property for the search space.

Reproduction Notes

If reproducing this paper, I would isolate the score from the search algorithm:

  1. Fix the CNN search space and mutation ranges first.
  2. Implement Zen-Score with residual links removed only during scoring.
  3. Verify Figure 2-like behavior on toy depth / width sweeps before running NAS.
  4. Compare Zen-Score against SynFlow, NASWOT, and TE-Score using the same EA loop.
  5. Count the actual latency predictor and hardware benchmark cost separately from score computation.
  6. Train the final selected networks with the same augmentation and teacher setup before comparing ImageNet top-1.

The important sanity check is step 3. If the score does not increase with width/depth in the expected controlled setting, the full search result is hard to trust.

Takeaway

Zen-NAS is a good example of a zero-shot NAS paper that pays attention to numerical details. The idea is not just “rank architectures without training”; it is “rank architectures with an expressivity proxy that survives BatchNorm and scale effects.”

I would use Zen-Score as a first-stage filter or search signal in a CNN space where the candidates share a regular macro grammar. I would be more cautious using it outside that setting. The paper’s main practical lesson is that a zero-shot proxy needs to be designed together with the search space and the deployment constraint.
