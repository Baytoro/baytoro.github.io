Paper: Learned Step Size Quantization

LSQ is a quantization-aware training paper that is easy to underestimate. At first glance, it only makes the quantization step size learnable. That sounds like a small change because many uniform quantizers already have a scale. The important part is not just that is trained. The important part is how the gradient to is defined and scaled.

The paper keeps the inference format simple: low-precision integer matrix multiplication followed by a scale multiplication. It does not learn an arbitrary codebook like LQ-Nets, and it does not rely on a post-training reconstruction objective like later PTQ work. LSQ’s bet is more conservative: keep the uniform integer grid, but let task loss tune the bin width.

Why LSQ Matters

Uniform quantization has one central parameter: the step size . If is too small, many values hit the clip boundary. If is too large, the grid is coarse and normal values suffer rounding error. Static calibration can choose a reasonable initial value, but QAT changes weights and activation distributions during training. A fixed scale is a bad place to hide a moving target.

LSQ treats the step size as a model parameter, one per weight layer and one per activation layer. The final runtime still looks like a standard scaled integer path:

Training Setup

The paper trains quantized models from a full-precision initialization. Full-precision weights are stored and updated, while quantized weights and quantized activations are used in the forward and backward computation. The first and last layers remain at 8-bit precision, a common choice in low-bit CNN QAT because those layers are often more sensitive.

The main ImageNet setup is:

  • pre-activation ResNet, VGG-16 with batch norm, and SqueezeNext architectures;
  • 2-bit, 3-bit, 4-bit, and 8-bit quantization for matrix multiplication layers except first and last;
  • 90 epochs for 2-, 3-, and 4-bit networks;
  • 1 epoch for 8-bit networks;
  • cosine learning-rate decay;
  • PyTorch implementation.

This is not post-training quantization. LSQ assumes a real QAT budget.

ImageNet Results

The main ImageNet comparison is dense, but the pattern is easy to read: LSQ is strongest in the 2-bit to 4-bit region, where step-size errors hurt most.

Some rows worth remembering:

  • ResNet-18 at 2-bit reaches 67.6 top-1, compared with a 70.5 full-precision baseline.
  • ResNet-18 at 4-bit reaches 71.1 top-1, slightly above the listed full-precision baseline.
  • ResNet-34 at 3-bit reaches 73.4 top-1, close to its 74.1 full-precision baseline.
  • ResNet-50 at 4-bit reaches 76.7 top-1, close to 76.9 full precision.
  • VGG-16bn at 3-bit reaches 73.4 top-1, matching the listed full-precision baseline.

The paper also notes that increasing from 4-bit to 8-bit often gives little or no additional accuracy in these settings. That does not mean 8-bit is useless. It means that with QAT and a learned step size, 4-bit can already be near the full-precision operating point for these CNNs.

That is a different question from “what is the best bit-width for one architecture?” If the deployment budget is model size, architecture and precision should be chosen together.

  • no scale can train at learning rate 0.0001, but only reaches 64.2;
  • multiplying the proposed scale by 10 or by 0.1 also loses accuracy.

This table is the core of the paper. If I had to summarize LSQ in one sentence, it would be: learn the quantization step size, but normalize its gradient so it behaves like a real parameter rather than a giant accumulator.

Knowledge Distillation

LSQ also combines well with same-architecture knowledge distillation:

The paper uses a full-precision teacher of the same architecture. The strongest claim is that 3-bit models reach full-precision accuracy in this setting:

  • ResNet-18 at 3-bit reaches 70.6 top-1, compared with 70.5 full precision.
  • ResNet-34 at 3-bit reaches 74.3, compared with 74.1 full precision.
  • ResNet-50 at 3-bit reaches 76.9, matching the 76.9 full-precision baseline.

This is an important distinction: LSQ alone already gives strong QAT results, and distillation further closes the gap for 3-bit models.

LSQ Does Not Minimize Quantization Error

One of the more interesting observations is that the learned step size is not the same as the step size that minimizes tensor reconstruction error. For a 2-bit ResNet-18, the paper compares the final learned against a grid of candidate values and checks mean absolute error, mean square error, and KL divergence.

The differences are large:

  • activation layers differ by 50 percent for mean absolute error, 63 percent for mean square error, and 64 percent for KL divergence;
  • weight layers differ by 47 percent for mean absolute error, 28 percent for mean square error, and 46 percent for KL divergence.

The interpretation is important: task-optimal quantization is not necessarily distribution-optimal quantization. A scale that minimizes may not minimize classification loss.

This is also why LSQ is a bridge between simple calibration and later reconstruction-based methods. Calibration chooses a scale from tensor statistics. LSQ lets the task loss choose.

Relation To PACT And LQ-Nets

LSQ sits between PACT and LQ-Nets.

PACT learns an activation clipping threshold. It improves activation range selection but does not learn a full weight and activation step-size system in the same way.

LQ-Nets learns a more flexible quantization basis. That can improve expressivity at low bit-width, but the resulting codebook is less obviously mapped to standard integer kernels.

LSQ chooses the middle path:

  • keep the uniform grid;
  • learn one scale per quantized layer tensor;
  • preserve a simple integer inference path;
  • spend the method complexity in the gradient, not the runtime format.

That is why LSQ became a common QAT baseline. It gives the optimizer a useful degree of freedom without changing the deployed arithmetic too much.

Reproduction Notes

If I were reproducing LSQ, I would log these signals:

  1. learned weight step size per layer;
  2. learned activation step size per layer;
  3. gradient norm of step sizes before and after scaling;
  4. clipping ratio for activations and weights;
  5. top-1 accuracy under the same weight decay sweep as Table 2.

The likely failure modes are straightforward:

  • forgetting to scale the step-size gradient;
  • using the wrong convention for signed weights;
  • letting first and last layers become too low precision;
  • treating LSQ as PTQ and expecting it to work without training;
  • comparing against a baseline with a mismatched training recipe.

The paper is compact, but the engineering lesson is durable. In low-bit QAT, the quantizer parameter is part of the model. If that parameter receives gradients on the wrong scale, the model may fail even when the forward quantizer is correct.

Takeaway

LSQ is not about inventing a new integer format. It is about making the standard uniform format trainable in a controlled way.

The part to remember is:

The step size receives gradients from many tensor elements, so the gradient must be normalized by layer size and quantization range. Once that is done, a very simple quantizer becomes a strong low-bit QAT baseline on ImageNet.
