ZeroQ: Data-Free Post-Training Quantization

Paper: ZeroQ: A Novel Zero Shot Quantization Framework

ZeroQ starts from a deployment constraint that is easy to forget: sometimes the model is available but the original calibration data is not. Data-free PTQ tries to quantize such a model without real samples.

Motivation

Ordinary PTQ needs a calibration set to estimate activation ranges and sometimes to reconstruct layer outputs. In privacy-sensitive or model-zoo settings, those samples may be unavailable. ZeroQ asks whether the network can generate its own calibration signal.

The paper uses synthetic data to estimate sensitivity and activation ranges. These images are not meant to look like natural images; they only need to trigger internal statistics similar to real data.

Methodology

The key source of information is BatchNorm. A trained BN layer stores running mean and variance. ZeroQ optimizes random inputs so that their intermediate activations match those stored statistics.

Results

ZeroQ reports CNN quantization results where data-free calibration approaches real-data PTQ in several 8-bit settings. At very low precision, synthetic data is less informative than real calibration data, but it is much better than blind min/max quantization.

Takeaway

ZeroQ is specific to BatchNorm-heavy networks. It does not directly solve data-free quantization for LayerNorm-only transformers. But the broader lesson matters: calibration data is a dependency. If the dependency is missing, internal model statistics are a possible substitute.

For LLMs, the analogous question is harder because there are no BN running statistics. Later LLM PTQ methods therefore usually assume at least a small text calibration set.

What Distilled Data Actually Provides

The generated images are not supposed to be semantically meaningful. They are a calibration probe. If the synthetic batch produces activation statistics similar to the training distribution, then it can estimate ranges and layer sensitivity.

This explains why BatchNorm is central. BN stores a small summary of the training distribution. ZeroQ turns that summary into an optimization target.

Failure Mode

The method becomes weaker when the model does not contain useful stored statistics. LayerNorm in transformers normalizes per sample and does not keep dataset running means/variances. That is why ZeroQ is not directly a modern LLM PTQ method.

Practical Takeaway

Data-free quantization is attractive for model zoos, privacy-sensitive deployments, and vendor-distributed checkpoints. But if even a few hundred real calibration samples are available, real data will usually provide a more faithful activation distribution than synthetic BN matching.

Practical Reading

The method works best in architectures where batch norm stores useful distribution statistics. That is a real limitation: Transformer models with LayerNorm do not provide the same free statistics. Later LLM PTQ methods therefore rely on small calibration text sets, synthetic-token ideas, or layer reconstruction instead of directly copying ZeroQ’s BN-matching recipe.

The useful concept is data-free calibration. If privacy, licensing, or deployment constraints prevent access to training data, quantization still needs some proxy for activation ranges and layer sensitivity. ZeroQ is one of the cleanest early formulations of that problem.

Experiment Reading

The quality of generated calibration data can be checked without labels. Feed the synthetic samples through the model and compare activation ranges with real calibration data if a small private validation set is available. If ranges match in early layers but diverge later, the generated data may be good enough for shallow quantization but weak for aggressive low-bit settings.

The paper also uses sensitivity to guide mixed precision. This matters because data-free quantization has two uncertainties at once: the calibration distribution is synthetic, and the bit allocation may be wrong. Sensitivity analysis gives a second signal to avoid spending too few bits on fragile layers.

ZeroQ is less directly applicable to LayerNorm-only Transformers, but the principle remains useful: when real calibration data is not available, generate or approximate the statistics the quantizer actually needs, instead of pretending min/max can be chosen blindly.
