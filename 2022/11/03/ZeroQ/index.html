ZeroQ: Data-Free Post-Training Quantization

H.J. Chen
  2022-11-03 2022-11-03 Created  
  • Work hard

ZeroQ is an early and very clean formulation of data-free post-training quantization. The deployment setting is simple: the pretrained model is available, but the original training or calibration data is not. A normal PTQ pipeline would use calibration samples to choose activation clipping ranges and to estimate which layers are fragile. ZeroQ tries to recover those signals from the model itself.

The key observation is that many CNN checkpoints contain BatchNorm layers, and BatchNorm stores running means and variances collected during training. Those statistics are not the original dataset, but they are a compact trace of the distribution that the model saw. ZeroQ turns that trace into synthetic calibration data.

Problem Setting

The paper is not about quantization-aware training. It is a post-training method:

  • No access to training or validation data.
  • No fine-tuning in the main results.
  • Uniform quantization is supported, but the stronger results come from mixed precision.
  • The target models are mostly BatchNorm-heavy CNNs: ResNet, MobileNetV2, ShuffleNet, SqueezeNext, InceptionV3, and RetinaNet with a ResNet50 backbone.

This matters because post-training quantization normally needs real samples. Without them, activation ranges are easy to misestimate, and layer sensitivity becomes guesswork. ZeroQ’s answer is to synthesize a small batch of “Distilled Data” and use it as a calibration probe.

Distilled Data From BatchNorm

ZeroQ starts from random Gaussian inputs and optimizes them so that their intermediate activations match the BatchNorm statistics stored in the model. The generated samples do not need to look like real images. They only need to excite the network in a way that exposes useful activation ranges and layer sensitivity.

The method is lightweight compared with training. The paper reports that generating 32 distilled images for ResNet50 on ImageNet takes about 3 seconds on an 8-V100 system. The complete ResNet50 quantization pipeline is reported as under 30 seconds.

Sensitivity Measurement

The next question is where to spend bits. Some layers can tolerate lower precision, while others cause large output drift if quantized aggressively. ZeroQ measures the sensitivity of layer at bit width by quantizing only that layer and comparing the output distribution with the full-precision model:

Here is not a real training image. It is distilled data. The metric is label-free, which is important because the zero-shot setting has neither labels nor input samples.

Figure 1 from the paper. The top branch is the original model; the bottom branch quantizes one layer. The KL divergence between the two outputs is used as the layer sensitivity.

The sensitivity curves are the most convincing part of the method. Gaussian data produces a very different sensitivity ranking, especially in early ResNet50 layers. Distilled data tracks the real training-data sensitivity much more closely.

Figure 2 from the paper. On the left, distilled data is much closer to training data than Gaussian noise. On the right, sensitivity rises sharply as weights move from 8-bit to 4-bit and 2-bit.

This explains why blindly using random Gaussian calibration is not enough. A synthetic batch is useful only if it produces the right internal activation behavior.

ImageNet Results

The main ImageNet table covers ResNet50, MobileNetV2, and ShuffleNet. The strongest message is that ZeroQ is almost lossless at W8A8, while still usable in much more aggressive mixed-precision settings.

Table I from the paper. “No D” means no real data is used, and “No FT” means no fine-tuning. “MP” means mixed-precision weights.

Some numbers worth remembering:

  • ResNet50 W8A8: 77.67 Top-1 versus 77.72 full-precision baseline.
  • ResNet50 mixed weights with 6-bit activations: 77.43 Top-1, higher than the OCS comparison row at 74.80.
  • ResNet50 aggressive mixed weights with 8-bit activations: 75.80 Top-1 at 12.17 MB.
  • MobileNetV2 W8A8: 72.91 Top-1 versus 73.03 baseline, and above DFQ’s 71.20 row in the same table.
  • ShuffleNet W8A8: 64.94 Top-1 versus 65.07 baseline.

The compact models are harder. MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNet lose more accuracy under aggressive average-4-bit weight compression, which is expected because there is less redundancy to absorb quantization noise.

Detection And Low-Bit Uniform Results

ZeroQ also tests RetinaNet on COCO. This is useful because object detection has a more complicated model graph than classification, and some RetinaNet layers are not directly followed by BN. The paper handles this by extracting the ResNet backbone statistics and then feeding distilled data through RetinaNet for sensitivity and range estimation.

Table II from the paper. W8A8 reaches the same 36.4 mAP as the full-precision baseline; the mixed-weight rows trade mAP for model size.

For RetinaNet, ZeroQ reports:

  • W8A8: 36.4 mAP, matching the full-precision baseline.
  • Mixed weights with 6-bit activations: 35.9 mAP.
  • Aggressive mixed weights with 8-bit activations: 33.7 mAP.

The ResNet18 table is also worth reading because it compares very low-bit uniform post-quantization settings against DFC and PACT.

Table III from the paper. ZeroQ remains competitive without fine-tuning, especially in the 6-bit and 8-bit rows.

The W4A4 case is difficult. ZeroQ with percentile clipping reaches 69.05 Top-1, while DFC without fine-tuning reports 55.49 in the same row group. At 6-bit and 8-bit, ZeroQ is very close to the 71.47 full-precision baseline.

Ablation: Why Distilled Data Matters

The distilled-data ablation asks whether BN-matched synthetic data is actually better than simple Gaussian data. It is.

Table IV from the paper. The quantized setting uses mixed weights and 8-bit activations.

For ResNet50, Gaussian data gives 75.44, training data gives 75.95, and distilled data gives 75.80. That is a small gap. For MobileNetV2, the difference is larger: Gaussian data gives 66.73, training data gives 69.06, and distilled data gives 68.83.

My read is that distilled data works because it recovers the statistics the quantizer actually needs. It is not a substitute for labels, and it is not a learned data generator. It is a calibration probe constructed from BN statistics.

Ablation: Why Sensitivity Allocation Matters

The sensitivity ablation is even more direct. If the bit allocation is random, the average result collapses. If the sensitivity objective is inverted, it also collapses. ZeroQ’s Pareto allocation is stable across the selected top configurations.

Table V from the paper. Under the average 4-bit weight constraint, ZeroQ’s best, worst, and mean configurations are all around 75.7 Top-1, while random and inverse choices fail badly.

This table is a good reminder that “mixed precision” is not automatically useful. It helps only when the allocation rule has a real signal.

Limitations

ZeroQ is not a generic answer to data-free quantization. Its strongest assumption is BatchNorm. A trained BN layer stores running statistics, and those statistics become the target for distilled-data optimization. LayerNorm-only Transformers do not provide the same dataset-level running mean and variance. That is why modern LLM PTQ methods usually rely on a small text calibration set, activation-aware heuristics, or reconstruction objectives instead of copying ZeroQ directly.

There is also a practical boundary around very compact models and very low precision. The W8A8 results are almost lossless, but aggressive average-4-bit settings on MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNet still lose noticeable accuracy. That is not a failure of the paper; it is the real tradeoff.

Finally, “zero-shot” does not mean “no information.” ZeroQ uses the weights, architecture, BatchNorm statistics, and the model’s own output distribution. It removes the data dependency, not the need for calibration signals.

Takeaway

ZeroQ’s useful idea is narrow but important: if real calibration data is unavailable, a model may still contain enough internal statistics to synthesize calibration probes. For BN-heavy CNNs, matching BatchNorm statistics gives synthetic data that is much better than Gaussian noise, and that data can drive both activation range selection and mixed-precision sensitivity analysis.

For LLMs, the direct recipe does not transfer cleanly because BatchNorm is absent. The broader lesson still transfers: quantization needs a distributional proxy. If the real calibration distribution is missing, the method must replace it with some trace that the model already carries, or with a carefully designed synthetic signal.
