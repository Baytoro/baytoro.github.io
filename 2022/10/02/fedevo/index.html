Real-time Federated Evolutionary Neural Architecture Search

H.J. Chen
  2022-10-02 2022-10-02 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 
  • | FL 

Paper : Real-time Federated Evolutionary Neural Architecture Search.

I will call the method FedEvo below. The interesting part of this paper is not that it uses evolutionary NAS again, but that it tries to make NAS happen inside the normal communication loop of federated learning. A conventional evolutionary NAS setup evaluates many candidate networks after training them, while federated learning already pays a high price for every communication round. If we naively combine the two, each client would have to download, train, and upload too many models.

The paper’s answer is simple: keep one shared master model, sample sub-models from it, train those sub-models on sampled clients, and use NSGA-II to select architectures under both accuracy and FLOPs constraints.

Motivation

Federated learning has two constraints that make centralized NAS awkward.

First, clients are usually small devices. Training a whole population of candidate networks on every client is unrealistic. Second, communication is the bottleneck. Sending a full model population, or retraining every offspring from scratch, quickly dominates the cost.

The paper also points out a less obvious issue: many NAS methods rely on transfer, proxy tasks, or centralized validation. In FL, data is distributed and often non-IID, so the architecture that looks good on one data split or one client group may not be stable globally.

So the target problem is:

Requirement Why it matters in FL
Search during training Avoid an expensive offline NAS stage.
Train only small sampled models Reduce local compute and upload size.
Reuse model weights Avoid reinitializing every offspring.
Optimize model size too Edge clients care about FLOPs, not only accuracy.

Contributions

The paper has three concrete contributions.

  1. Double sampling. For every evolutionary generation, the server samples both architectures and participating clients. A client trains only one sub-model in that generation instead of a full population.
  2. Sub-model aggregation. Uploaded sub-models are reconstructed back into the master-model parameter space. Trained branches use client updates; missing branches are filled from the previous master model before aggregation.
  3. Online multi-objective search. One generation of NSGA-II is aligned with one communication round. The objectives are test error and FLOPs (the paper reports FLOPs as MACs).

Search Space

The master network is a small CNN for CIFAR-10. It starts with a convolution block, then uses 12 choice blocks grouped by channel width: 64, 128, 256, and 512. Each choice block has four candidate branches.

Each choice block chooses one of four candidates:

  • identity block
  • residual block
  • inverted residual block
  • depthwise separable block

Because each choice block has four options, it can be encoded with 2 bits. With 12 choice blocks, an architecture choice key has 24 bits. This is a small search space by modern NAS standards, but it is enough for the paper’s point: reduce federated compute and communication while still searching over meaningful CNN blocks.

Double Sampling

The double-sampling step has two levels.

First, the server samples one sub-model from the master model according to a choice key. Instead of sending the whole supernet, the client receives a concrete path through the choice blocks.

Second, the server samples clients. If there are total clients and the client participation ratio is , then the round samples clients. These clients are split over the evolutionary population of size , so one candidate is trained by roughly clients.

This matters because each client sees only one sub-model per generation. The local workload becomes bounded by a single sampled network, not by the population size.

Aggregation

Aggregation is the part I found most useful in this paper. A client only trains the branches that exist in its sampled sub-model, but the server still needs to update one master model.

The paper handles this by reconstructing a full master-shaped model for each upload:

  • branches selected by the client use the trained client weights;
  • branches not selected by that client are copied from the previous master model;
  • reconstructed models are averaged, weighted by client data size.

This is basically weight sharing plus federated averaging, but applied at the branch level. It avoids random reinitialization of offspring models, which is one reason the real-time version is more stable than the offline evolutionary baseline in the paper.

NSGA-II Objective

FedEvo uses NSGA-II to keep a Pareto set instead of choosing one scalar reward. The two objectives are:

Objective Meaning
Test error Lower is better. Used as the accuracy-side fitness.
FLOPs (MAC) Lower is better. Used as the efficiency-side fitness.

The paper then marks two representative solutions on the Pareto front:

  • High: the solution with the highest accuracy;
  • Knee: the solution near the knee point, usually a better accuracy/FLOPs tradeoff.

One small caveat: the paper writes “test error” as the fitness. In a production FL setup, I would expect this to be a validation metric or a server-side held-out evaluation, otherwise the test set becomes part of model selection.

Experiments

The experiments are on CIFAR-10 with 50,000 training images and 10,000 test images. The paper simulates 10, 20, and 50 clients under both IID and non-IID splits. For non-IID, each client has images from 5 classes.

Important settings:

Item Value
Dataset CIFAR-10
Clients 10, 20, 50
Local epoch 1
Train batch size 50
NSGA-II population 10
Generations / rounds 500
Baseline ResNet18 for CIFAR-10
Baseline FLOPs 0.5587G MAC

For 10 and 20 clients, the best evolved model usually beats ResNet18 while using fewer FLOPs. The knee solution is more aggressive: it gives up some accuracy but cuts computation heavily. The 50-client case is weaker. The paper explains this as a data-per-client issue: when total data is fixed, more clients means less local data per client, so it becomes harder to find a stable global model.

Real-time Behavior

The real-time curves for 20 clients are the most convincing part of the experiments.

At the beginning, ResNet18 is better. That is expected: the searched models are still being optimized while the master model is changing. After about 200 communication rounds, the selected high-accuracy and knee solutions catch up and pass ResNet18 in the reported 20-client settings.

The FLOPs curves also show the point of multi-objective search. The knee solution stays far below ResNet18 in compute, while the high-accuracy solution fluctuates more because it is chasing accuracy during the search.

The paper also compares against an offline evolutionary federated NAS baseline. In that baseline, the selected model is trained from scratch for 500 communication rounds. The reported runtime is about 16.1 hours/GPU for real-time NAS over 500 generations, versus about 6.55 hours/GPU for only 50 offline generations before retraining cost. The paper summarizes this as roughly a 5x speedup per search round for the real-time method.

Takeaways

I like this paper because it treats communication rounds as the unit of NAS cost. Many NAS papers hide the search cost behind a centralized proxy task; here, the search loop is forced to live inside FL.

What I would keep from this paper:

  • Represent architectures with compact choice keys.
  • Do not ask each client to train a population.
  • Aggregate partial sub-model updates back into a shared master model.
  • Optimize compute explicitly, not as an afterthought.

What feels limited:

  • The search space is small and CNN-specific.
  • The experiments are only on CIFAR-10.
  • The 50-client results show that the method is sensitive to data fragmentation.
  • Using “test error” as the search fitness is not ideal if interpreted literally.

Overall, FedEvo is a useful early design for federated NAS: it is not the most powerful NAS method, but it gives a concrete recipe for making evolutionary search compatible with FL communication constraints.
