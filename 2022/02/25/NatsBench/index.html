Paper: NATS-Bench: Benchmarking NAS Algorithms for Architecture Topology and Size

Code and data: https://xuanyidong.com/assets/projects/NATS-Bench

NATS-Bench is a NAS benchmark paper, not a new search algorithm. Its main contribution is to make two usually mixed questions separable:

  • Is a NAS method good at searching cell topology?
  • Is it good at searching architecture size, especially channel allocation?

This distinction matters because architecture quality is not only a graph problem. Width, channel schedule, FLOPs, and training recipe can move accuracy as much as the cell topology. If two NAS papers use different search spaces and retraining setups, it is hard to tell whether the search algorithm improved or whether the evaluation protocol changed.

Search Spaces

NATS-Bench defines two spaces, both under the same macro skeleton.

The topology space searches the operation assigned to each edge in a 4-node cell. The operation set has 5 choices:

  • zeroize
  • skip connection
  • 1x1 convolution
  • 3x3 convolution
  • 3x3 average pooling

This gives candidate cells. Each architecture in stacks the searched cell in the fixed macro skeleton.

With 5 channel decisions, this gives candidates. This is the part I find most useful: it makes width and capacity allocation a first-class NAS benchmark instead of treating them as post-search tuning.

Compared With NAS-Bench-101

The paper positions NATS-Bench as broader than NAS-Bench-101 in three ways: more datasets, more diagnostic information, and an additional size search space.

NAS-Bench-101 has many more unique architectures, but only one dataset and a topology-style space. NATS-Bench is smaller in topology count but evaluates both and on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet16-120. It also provides fine-grained information such as accuracy/loss curves, parameter counts, and FLOPs.

That makes the benchmark useful for more than ranking final methods. It can also answer diagnostic questions:

  • Does validation ranking track test ranking?
  • Does a good architecture on CIFAR-10 transfer to CIFAR-100 or ImageNet16-120?
  • Does a method behave differently on topology search and size search?
  • Does an algorithm beat simple baselines under the same query budget?

What Is Stored

For each candidate, NATS-Bench stores training logs and evaluation metrics instead of only final test accuracy. The paper lists training accuracy, test accuracy, training loss, test loss, parameters, FLOPs, latency-like cost information, and trained parameters for each architecture/run.

The practical effect is that a NAS algorithm can query a benchmark table instead of retraining each sampled architecture. That makes repeated algorithm comparison feasible, especially for methods where the search itself must be run many times to estimate variance.

The authors evaluate architectures under a unified split and training setup on:

  • CIFAR-10
  • CIFAR-100
  • ImageNet16-120

This is still small-image classification, but using three datasets is important. A benchmark that only rewards a CIFAR-10-specific architecture can quietly encourage overfitting to that one table.

Dataset Ranking Behavior

The first diagnostic result checks how architecture rankings transfer across datasets.

The left plot is the topology search space. Rankings are sorted by CIFAR-10, then plotted against CIFAR-100 and ImageNet16-120. The trend is fairly diagonal, which means topology rankings are somewhat consistent across datasets.

The right plot is the size search space. The scatter is much wider. That is the strongest motivation for having at all: the best channel allocation is more dataset-dependent than the best topology under this benchmark.

Performance Landscape

The full performance landscape is also useful. It shows training/test accuracy against parameters and FLOPs for every candidate.

Two points stand out:

  1. Architectures with similar parameter counts or FLOPs can have very different accuracy.
  2. Human-designed reference patterns such as ResNet or pyramid channel schedules are competitive, but not always Pareto optimal.

For topology search, the vertical bands reflect discrete operation choices and fixed macro sizes. For size search, the bands and curved fronts make channel allocation visible. This is exactly the sort of structure a NAS optimizer can exploit or overfit.

Validation And Test Correlation

The benchmark depends on validation accuracy being a reasonable search signal. If validation and test rankings were unrelated, a NAS method that queries validation performance would not be meaningful.

The paper reports that validation rankings and test rankings are similar in both spaces. That supports the usual NAS workflow: search on validation, then report test performance of the selected architecture.

But this should not be read as “validation is perfect.” The correlation is strong enough for benchmark use, while still leaving room for methods to overfit validation quirks.

Cross-Dataset Transfer

The Kendall rank correlation heatmaps show a more nuanced picture.

For , the all-candidate rank correlations are high. But when the paper only keeps high-accuracy candidates, correlations drop. For , the correlations are weaker, especially when focusing on top candidates.

This is a good warning for NAS papers. A method that selects the best architecture for one dataset may not transfer cleanly to another. The top of the ranking is exactly where NAS methods operate, and that region is noisier than the whole-space average suggests.

Search Curves: Multi-Trial Methods

The paper benchmarks 13 NAS algorithms. It separates methods that train/evaluate many candidates from weight-sharing methods.

For multi-trial methods, the comparison includes REA, REINFORCE, RANDOM, and BOHB. The curves are best read as best-found architecture quality over estimated wall-clock search time. REA and BOHB are strong in many settings, but the exact ordering depends on dataset and search space.

The important benchmark lesson is not “always use one method.” It is that the same algorithms can be compared under the same architecture table, training recipe, and repeated-run protocol.

Search Curves: Weight Sharing

Weight-sharing methods are evaluated separately because they use a supernet and do not pay full training cost for every sampled architecture.

The topology-space curves show instability for several weight-sharing methods. DARTS variants and GDAS can swing heavily before converging or failing. In the size-search curves, channel-wise interpolation is much more stable than masking-based strategies in the paper’s implementation.

This matches a common NAS observation: weight sharing buys search speed, but it can distort the ranking signal. As the search space grows, a shared set of weights may not represent each candidate accurately enough.

The gap between searched methods and the optimal row is the room a better search method could still use. The gap between weight-sharing and multi-trial methods is also a reminder that fast search is not automatically accurate search.

Training Budget Sensitivity

The appendix includes an interesting sanity check: lower-fidelity training can change the search dynamics.

For REA on CIFAR-10, using fewer training epochs can converge faster at the beginning but may not dominate after enough search time. This is a standard multi-fidelity tradeoff. Short training is cheap and informative early, but it can bias the ranking of final architectures.

For benchmark users, this means that “query cost” should be part of the experimental claim. A method that wins under cheap proxy training may not win when the benchmark asks for higher-fidelity estimates.

How I Would Use It

NATS-Bench is good for algorithm diagnosis:

  • Compare search methods under a controlled query budget.
  • Plot best-so-far curves instead of reporting only the final architecture.
  • Separate topology search from channel-size search.
  • Check variance across repeated runs.
  • Test whether a method’s ranking behavior transfers across datasets.

It is less suitable as a final deployment benchmark:

  • The tasks are small image-classification datasets.
  • The architectures are CNN-style cell networks, not modern ViTs or LLMs.
  • Hardware latency is not the main objective, even though FLOPs and cost metadata are available.
  • Since all architecture results are tabulated, benchmark overfitting is a real risk.

The paper explicitly discusses this risk. If a new NAS method uses operation-specific rules designed after inspecting the best NATS-Bench architecture, it can look strong on the table without generalizing. A fair use of NATS-Bench should keep the algorithm generic and report multiple runs.

Takeaway

NATS-Bench is valuable because it expands NAS benchmarking beyond topology search. The topology space is useful for comparing cell-search algorithms, while the size space exposes how important channel allocation can be.

The strongest lesson for me is that NAS benchmarks should be diagnostic, not just leaderboards. Search curves, cross-dataset ranking, validation/test correlation, FLOPs/parameter landscapes, and repeated-run variance tell more about an algorithm than one final accuracy number.
