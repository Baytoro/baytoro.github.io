NAS-Bench-301 and the Case for Surrogate NAS Benchmarks

H.J. Chen
  2022-01-18 2022-01-18 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • NAS 
  • | Benchmark 

Siems J, Zimmer L, Zela A, Lukasik J, Keuper M, Hutter F. NAS-Bench-301 and the Case for Surrogate Benchmarks for Neural Architecture Search. ICLR 2022.

  • Paper: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.09777.pdf
  • Code: https://github.com/automl/nasbench301

NAS-Bench-301 attacks the scaling limit of tabular NAS benchmarks. NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-201 are exact lookup tables, which is excellent for reproducibility, but their spaces are deliberately small. Real NAS spaces such as DARTS or FBNet can contain to architectures. Exhaustive training is impossible.

The paper’s solution is a surrogate NAS benchmark. Instead of evaluating every architecture, it samples a large but finite set of architectures, trains them with a fixed recipe, fits performance predictors, and uses those predictors as cheap benchmark oracles.

The tradeoff is clear: a tabular benchmark is exact but small; a surrogate benchmark is approximate but much larger.

Why Surrogate Benchmarks

The motivating experiment uses NAS-Bench-101 itself. A graph neural network surrogate is trained on subsets of the NAS-Bench-101 table and compared with single tabular observations. Once enough architectures are used for training, the surrogate can beat a one-seed table lookup because it smooths out evaluation noise.

The crossover happens around 21,500 NAS-Bench-101 architectures in the paper’s experiment. That is the conceptual hook: if architecture evaluations are noisy, a learned predictor can sometimes provide a better estimate of expected performance than a single real training run.

This does not mean surrogates are automatically safe. It means they can be useful if their ranking behavior, uncertainty, and optimizer trajectories are validated carefully.

Benchmark Construction

The general pipeline is:

  1. Choose a large search space, such as DARTS or FBNet.
  2. Sample architectures with a mixture of random search and NAS optimizers.
  3. Train and evaluate those architectures with a controlled recipe.
  4. Fit surrogate models from architecture encoding to validation performance.
  5. Validate the surrogates with error metrics, rank metrics, and optimizer trajectory tests.
  6. Release both the benchmark oracle and enough data to audit it.

The main dataset is SNB-DARTS, built on the DARTS search space and CIFAR-10. The paper collects roughly 60k architectures. The sampling mixture includes random search and optimizer traces from methods such as BANANAS, DARTS, PC-DARTS, GDAS, DrNAS, regularized evolution, TPE, differential evolution, and COMBO.

The second dataset is based on FBNet and CIFAR-100. The FBNet space is even larger: the paper describes 22 searchable layers, each with 9 operation choices, giving roughly possible architectures, around .

Surrogate Model Choice

The paper compares several surrogate families, including graph neural networks and gradient-boosted trees. The strongest models include graph isomorphism networks, LightGBM-style boosting, and XGBoost-style boosting.

The authors evaluate both prediction quality and ranking quality. Mean absolute error is useful, but for NAS the ranking is often more important. A surrogate that predicts exact errors poorly but preserves the ordering of architectures can still be useful for optimizer comparison. Conversely, a low average error can hide bad behavior in the high-performing tail.

The noise-modeling table is important because architecture evaluations are stochastic. The paper compares a tabular single-observation baseline with learned surrogate ensembles on 500 architectures evaluated five times.

The GIN surrogate has the best MAE in this table and a much better KL divergence than the boosting models, suggesting better uncertainty modeling. This is one reason the paper does not rely on a single surrogate type. Multiple surrogate families reduce the chance that a NAS optimizer simply exploits one model’s bias.

True Benchmark vs Surrogate Trajectories

The central validation question is not only “does the surrogate predict held-out architectures?” It is “does an optimizer behave similarly on the surrogate as it does on the expensive real benchmark?”

The figure compares trajectories on the real benchmark with trajectories on GIN and XGBoost surrogate benchmarks trained with optimizer data. The broad convergence behavior is preserved. BANANAS improves strongly, random search stagnates, and the surrogate trajectories follow the same qualitative ordering.

The paper then repeats the experiment using surrogates trained only on random-search data.

Random-only training still gives plausible trajectories, but it is weaker in the best-performing regions. That makes sense: random sampling covers the global space, but strong optimizers concentrate in small regions that random search may undersample. A robust surrogate benchmark needs broad coverage and enough data near both good and bad boundaries.

Optimizer Data Distribution

The appendix gives an ECDF of validation error by optimizer.

The distribution is not uniform across optimizers. BANANAS samples many strong architectures; PC-DARTS, DrNAS, differential evolution, GDAS, and regularized evolution occupy different quality regions; TPE is only slightly better than random search in this plot; COMBO and DARTS include many weak architectures. This is useful for training surrogates because different optimizers expose different parts of the space.

The t-SNE visualization makes the coverage issue more concrete.

Random search covers the main cloud, while individual optimizers often explore specific regions. COMBO goes into areas less covered by random search. BANANAS concentrates outside the main cloud. Differential evolution finds a strong central region. This explains why leave-one-optimizer-out validation is necessary: a surrogate trained on one distribution may not extrapolate well to another optimizer’s candidates.

Leave-One-Optimizer-Out

The paper validates this directly with leave-one-optimizer-out experiments. The surrogate is trained without data from one optimizer and then tested on that held-out optimizer’s architectures.

The table shows a pattern that is easy to miss if one only reports average . Leaving out DARTS can hurt badly because DARTS contributes unusual skip-heavy architectures, but sparse Kendall tau can remain reasonably high. In other words, the surrogate may still rank bad DARTS architectures as bad even if it cannot predict exactly how bad they are.

The trajectory plot gives the practical version of the same test.

This is the kind of validation a surrogate benchmark needs. If a new optimizer searches in a region not represented in the training data, the surrogate may be overconfident or wrong. Holding out optimizer families is a stronger test than a random train/test split.

FBNet Surrogate Benchmark

The paper also constructs a surrogate benchmark for the FBNet search space on CIFAR-100. Here the authors use an XGBoost surrogate trained on randomly sampled architectures. The scaling plot shows that more training data reduces MAE and improves rank correlation.

With transferred hyperparameters from the DARTS surrogate, the FBNet surrogate reaches about and sparse Kendall tau around 0.75 on the test set. That is not perfect, but it is enough to reproduce broad optimizer trajectories.

This part of the paper is important because it argues that surrogate NAS benchmarks are not a one-off trick for DARTS. The same methodology can be applied to a different, much larger search space, as long as the data collection and validation protocol are careful.

Failure Cases and Boundary Checks

The paper does not pretend that surrogates are exact. It studies a constrained DARTS failure case where DARTS can diverge toward cells with only skip connections.

The surrogate can capture the broad divergent trajectory, especially when the relevant data is included. But uncertainty grows when the surrogate is asked to extrapolate into regions it has not seen. This is the main operational risk of surrogate benchmarks: a NAS method can optimize the benchmark model rather than the real architecture performance.

The parameter-free operation analysis is another useful boundary test.

The ground truth and surrogate predictions both show that too many parameter-free operations, especially skip connections, lead to worse validation error. This is exactly the kind of degenerate region that differentiable NAS methods can exploit if the benchmark or search objective is not robust.

The one-shot optimizer trajectory plot checks whether the surrogate can monitor methods such as DARTS, PC-DARTS, and GDAS through the search process.

The curves are not a perfect replacement for real training, but they are informative enough to diagnose behavior at much lower cost.

How I Would Use It

For benchmarking a new NAS method, I would use NAS-Bench-301 differently from a tabular benchmark:

  1. Report both GIN and XGBoost surrogate results when possible.
  2. Use validation performance during search and reserve final real training checks for selected candidates.
  3. Track uncertainty or at least avoid making claims from a single surrogate prediction.
  4. Compare against random search, regularized evolution, BANANAS, and simple Bayesian optimization baselines.
  5. Inspect whether the method visits regions covered by the surrogate training data.
  6. Run a small number of real evaluations for final architectures if the claim is about actual model quality.
  7. Treat surprising superhuman surrogate scores as suspicious until verified.

The last point is the most important. A surrogate benchmark is a model. It can be exploited.

Limitations

NAS-Bench-301 expands the search space dramatically, but it introduces new limitations:

  • surrogate predictions are approximate;
  • training data coverage affects every conclusion;
  • strong optimizers can search out-of-distribution regions;
  • random train/test splits can overestimate reliability;
  • one surrogate family can bias the benchmark;
  • final deployment claims still need real training and hardware checks.

These limitations are not a reason to avoid the benchmark. They define the right level of evidence. NAS-Bench-301 is excellent for comparing optimizer behavior in a large search space. It is not a final oracle for architecture quality.

Takeaway

NAS-Bench-301 is the bridge between exact tabular NAS benchmarks and expensive real architecture search. It keeps the reproducibility benefit of benchmarks while moving into search spaces that are orders of magnitude larger than NAS-Bench-101 or NAS-Bench-201. The price is approximation, so the benchmark must be used with surrogate validation, optimizer holdout tests, and real checks for final claims.
