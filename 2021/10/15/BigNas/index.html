BigNAS: Scaling Up Neural Architecture Search with Big Single-Stage Models

H.J. Chen
  2021-10-15 2021-10-15 Created  
  • Work hard 
  • >
  • PaperReading 
 
  • NAS 
  • | Mobile 

Yu J, Jin P, Liu H, et al. BigNAS: Scaling Up Neural Architecture Search with Big Single-Stage Models. ECCV 2020.

Paper

BigNAS is a one-shot NAS paper about making the supernet itself good enough for deployment. The claim is stronger than “use a supernet to rank candidates”: after training one single-stage model on ImageNet, child models from about 200 MFLOPs to 1 GFLOP can be sliced out and used directly, without retraining, finetuning, or other post-processing.

That is the part worth reading carefully. In many weight-sharing NAS methods, inherited accuracy is only a rough ranking signal and the final architecture still needs stand-alone training. BigNAS tries to remove that last step. The paper is therefore less about a new search algorithm and more about a training recipe that makes shared weights behave like a family of pretrained models.

Motivation

The workflow difference is simple:

Traditional one-shot methods train a supernet, search a path, then retrain or finetune the selected child model. OFA-style progressive methods make this more reusable, but still train the elastic model in stages. BigNAS asks whether all child models can be trained simultaneously in one stage and then deployed as-is.

The practical reason is deployment cost. If every target latency, FLOP budget, or device class requires another finetuning or retraining pass, the “cheap” NAS loop still leaves a long tail of engineering work. BigNAS wants the output to be a model family, not just an architecture list.

Search Space

BigNAS uses a MobileNetV2-style MBConv search space. The searchable dimensions are input resolution, channel width, depth, and kernel size; expansion ratio is mostly fixed by the MBConv type in the table.

The important details:

  • Training samples input resolutions from {192, 224, 288, 320}.
  • The supernet contains stage-wise choices for channel count, number of layers, and kernel size.
  • The reported BigNASModels are selected from roughly 200M to 1000M FLOPs, while the single-stage model covers a wider range during training.
  • The paper also notes that after batch-norm calibration, the trained model can generalize to input resolutions not used during training, such as 208, 240, 256, 272, 304, or 336.

This is not a cell-level NAS search space. It is closer to an elastic mobile network family: same backbone grammar, many possible widths/depths/kernels/resolutions.

Single-Stage Training

Sandwich Rule And Inplace Distillation

BigNAS inherits two useful ideas from Once-for-All:

  1. Sandwich rule: in each step, train the largest child, the smallest child, and sampled intermediate children.
  2. Inplace distillation: use the largest child model as the teacher for smaller child models in the same supernet.

This makes sense for the BigNAS goal. The largest child receives normal supervised training, while smaller children learn from a teacher that shares the same weights. The risky part is optimization: small and large child models do not converge at the same speed.

The left plot shows the core dilemma. The big child peaks earlier; the small child keeps improving later. If training stops early, small children are undertrained. If training continues with a decaying learning rate all the way down, the big child can overfit while the small child catches up.

Their fix is deliberately small: exponential decay with a constant ending. Once the learning rate reaches a floor, it stays there until the end. In the main ImageNet setting, the paper uses a 5% floor of the initial learning rate.

The appendix sweep is also useful:

LR floor Smallest model Biggest model Average
3% 76.5 80.8 78.7
5% 76.4 81.1 78.8
8% 76.3 81.3 78.8
10% 76.3 81.3 78.8

The exact floor is not the whole story, but keeping a non-zero ending learning rate is part of making both ends of the supernet usable.

Initialization And Skip Transitions

The paper spends real space on initialization because the single-stage model changes channel counts and fan-in across child models. A fixed-network initialization rule is not enough when the same residual block can be evaluated under many widths.

The BigNAS modification sets the learnable scaling coefficient in the last batch-norm layer of each residual block. This makes the residual branch initially output zeros before the skip connection, so the block starts close to an identity mapping. The paper says this initialization gives around +0.2% in the fixed-network setting, but is much more critical here, with roughly +1.0% improvement in their single-stage setting.

The early-training plot explains why. Naive initialization at the full learning rate can collapse; reducing the learning rate helps but slows training. Modified initialization lets the model keep the original learning rate and still learn quickly.

They also add explicit skip connections at stage transitions. When resolution or channel dimensions differ, the skip path uses 2x2 average pooling and/or 1x1 convolution. This keeps the identity path meaningful even when child models choose different stage configurations.

After the modifications, the small child improves more steadily and the large child avoids the earlier degradation. This figure is more convincing than just reporting the final architecture search result, because it shows the recipe improving the shared-weight training dynamics directly.

Regularization Only On The Full Model

BigNAS also changes where regularization is applied. A naive recipe applies dropout and weight decay to every sampled child model. BigNAS instead applies regularization only when training the largest/full network.

The reason is capacity mismatch. Small child models are already constrained; regularizing them like the largest model can underfit them. In the paper’s ablation, this modified rule improves the small child by about +0.5 top-1 and the big child by about +0.2.

The ImageNet training setup is still heavy: RMSProp, batch size 4096 on TPUs, AutoAugment, Swish, batch-norm post-calibration, weight decay , dropout 0.2 on the full model, and roughly 36 hours of training in the authors’ setup.

Architecture Selection

After training the single-stage model, BigNAS does not run a complicated controller. It uses a coarse-to-fine selection procedure:

First it evaluates coarse skeletons across resolution, depth, width, and kernel choices. Then it picks promising skeletons and mutates them with a finer grid under the target resource budget. The paper uses FLOPs for the reported selection because FLOPs are easier to reproduce than device-specific latency, but it notes that the evaluation metric could also be latency, memory footprint, or predictive accuracy on a target device.

One detail I like: since ImageNet validation is treated as the public “test set” in this community, they use predictive accuracy on the training set for selection after supernet training. That is not a perfect deployment protocol, but it avoids repeatedly tuning on the validation set.

Main Results

The headline result is the Pareto curve and comparison table:

Compactly, the BigNAS family looks like this:

Model Params FLOPs Top-1
BigNASModel-S 4.5M 242M 76.5
BigNASModel-M 5.5M 418M 78.9
BigNASModel-L 6.4M 586M 79.5
BigNASModel-XL 9.5M 1040M 80.9

The comparisons that matter most are compact enough to write directly:

  • Around 200M FLOPs, BigNASModel-S reaches 76.5 top-1 at 242M FLOPs, slightly above Once-for-All finetuned at 76.4 top-1 with 230M FLOPs.
  • Around 400M FLOPs, BigNASModel-M reaches 78.9 top-1 at 418M FLOPs, above EfficientNet-B0 at 77.3 top-1 with 390M FLOPs.
  • Around 600M FLOPs, BigNASModel-L reaches 79.5 top-1 at 586M FLOPs, above EfficientNet-B1 at 79.2 top-1 with 734M FLOPs.
  • Around 1000M FLOPs, BigNASModel-XL reaches 80.9 top-1 at 1040M FLOPs, above EfficientNet-B2 at 80.3 top-1 with 1050M FLOPs.

This table should not be read as a perfectly fair training recipe comparison. The paper itself notes that MNasNet and MobileNetV3 are mainly optimized for on-device latency instead of FLOPs, and different model families use different training hyperparameters. Still, the result is strong for the paper’s central claim: directly sliced child models are competitive without an extra training pass.

Does Finetuning Help?

For me, this is the most important evidence in the paper.

Table 2 tries to finetune selected child models for another 25 epochs with constant learning rates. A too-large finetuning LR hurts badly. A smaller LR mostly changes nothing. For example:

Model Direct slice Finetune LR 0.001 Finetune LR 0.0001
BigNASModel-S 76.5 76.4 76.5
BigNASModel-M 78.9 78.8 78.8
BigNASModel-L 79.5 79.6 79.7
BigNASModel-XL 80.9 80.6 80.8

Table 3 trains the selected architectures from scratch. Even with the better distillation variant, training from scratch remains slightly behind the directly sliced model:

Model Direct slice From scratch From scratch + distill B
BigNASModel-S 76.5 75.3 76.3
BigNASModel-M 78.9 77.4 78.6
BigNASModel-L 79.5 78.2 79.2
BigNASModel-XL 80.9 79.3 80.4

This is the key point. BigNAS is not only saying “our search found good architectures”. It is saying the weight-sharing model has already trained those architectures well enough that the inherited weights beat or match the usual post-processing baselines.

Relation To OFA

BigNAS is close to Once-for-All in spirit: both want one trained network to provide many deployable subnets. The difference is how much staging the training pipeline needs.

Method Training style Deployment claim
Once-for-All Progressive shrinking over elastic dimensions Subnets can be specialized and selected after elastic training
BigNAS Simultaneous single-stage training with a stronger recipe Sliced child models are directly deployable without retraining or finetuning

BigNAS still borrows OFA’s sandwich rule and inplace distillation. The new claim is that progressive shrinking is not strictly necessary if initialization, skip transitions, learning-rate schedule, and regularization are tuned for a very large single-stage model.

Limitations

The result is useful, but it is not a generic proof that all one-shot NAS supernets are deployable.

  • The search space is manually designed around MBConv mobile CNNs. It does not automatically cover arbitrary operators, attention blocks, or hardware-specific kernels.
  • The recipe is expensive: ImageNet-scale supernet training with batch size 4096 on TPUs is still a serious compute budget.
  • The final comparison is mostly FLOP-based. Real deployment often cares about latency, memory bandwidth, activation size, kernel availability, and device-specific scheduling.
  • The method depends on a carefully tuned training recipe. The paper’s strongest message is that supernet training quality matters; it does not make weight sharing automatically reliable.
  • Batch-norm calibration is still needed when slicing and evaluating child models, especially across different input resolutions.

Takeaway

BigNAS is valuable because it changes the question from “which architecture should NAS pick?” to “can a single trained supernet already be the pretrained model family?” The paper’s strongest evidence is Table 2 and Table 3: finetuning and retraining do not meaningfully improve the selected child models.

My main takeaway is that one-shot NAS fails or succeeds mostly at the supernet-training level. Search over a weak shared-weight model is ranking noise; search over a well-trained elastic model can produce deployable subnets directly. BigNAS is one of the cleaner demonstrations of that idea for mobile CNNs.
