BRECQ: Pushing the Limit of PTQ by Block Reconstruction

H.J. Chen
  2021-10-10 2021-10-10 Created  
  • Work hard 
  • >

Paper / Code

BRECQ is a useful CNN PTQ paper to read before looking at later LLM quantization work. The paper is not only about collecting activation ranges. It frames PTQ as a local reconstruction problem: given a small calibration set, optimize the quantized model so its internal computation stays close to the full-precision model.

The key design choice is the reconstruction unit. Layer-wise reconstruction is too local for modern residual networks. Whole-network reconstruction is too large and easy to overfit when only a small calibration set is available. BRECQ argues that a block is the practical middle point.

Reconstruction Granularity

Figure cropped from the paper PDF: network, stage, block, and layer granularities, together with the corresponding Hessian/Fisher block structure.

The figure explains why the paper prefers block reconstruction. In a residual CNN, a layer does not act alone. Convolutions, nonlinearities, batch norm, and skip connections form a functional block. If PTQ optimizes one layer at a time, it ignores error compensation inside the block.

BRECQ compares four granularities:

Granularity What it optimizes Practical issue
Layer-wise one layer output at a time misses cross-layer dependency
Block-wise one residual/building block balances dependency and overfitting
Stage-wise a group of blocks at the same resolution larger search space and harder calibration
Net-wise final network output overfits easily with limited calibration

The Hessian diagram is the paper’s intuition: layer-wise PTQ keeps only the layer-diagonal part, while full-network reconstruction tries to use the full matrix. Block reconstruction keeps the stronger intra-block interactions and drops weaker inter-block terms.

Optimization Loop

BRECQ optimizes block by block, using calibration inputs, full-precision block outputs, gradients, and optional activation step-size updates.

The procedure is still post-training quantization. Labels are not required, and the model is not trained end to end. For each block, BRECQ records calibration input, the full-precision output, and gradient information. It then optimizes the quantized block output to match the full-precision block output.

This is the same practical spirit as AdaRound, but the reconstruction target is a block rather than a single layer. The block gives internal layers room to absorb each other’s quantization errors.

Why Block, Not Layer

The block granularity is the best point in this ablation, especially for MobileNetV2.

The ablation is small but important. With 2-bit weight quantization, block reconstruction beats layer, stage, and net reconstruction on both ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2. The MobileNetV2 gap is especially clear: block reconstruction reaches 59.67, while layer-wise reconstruction reaches 52.13 and net-wise reconstruction collapses to 40.76.

This also explains why “larger reconstruction unit” is not automatically better. Network-wise optimization has the most dependency information, but with only a small calibration set it can fit the calibration examples instead of improving generalization.

Low-bit Results

Weight-only ImageNet results. BRECQ remains usable even at 2-bit weights with 32-bit activations.

For weight-only quantization, BRECQ is much stronger than range-only or layer-only PTQ baselines. On ResNet-18, the 2/32 result is 66.30 compared with 71.08 full precision. On MobileNetV2, 2/32 reaches 59.67 compared with 72.49 full precision, which is a meaningful result for such an aggressive setting.

Fully quantized ImageNet results. Activations are quantized too, which makes the setting much harder.

The fully quantized table is the more deployment-relevant one. At 4/4, BRECQ gives strong results across ResNet, MobileNetV2, RegNet, and MNasNet-style models. At 2/4, most previous PTQ methods fail badly, while BRECQ still produces usable accuracy.

Two details are worth keeping in mind when reading the table:

  • The first and last layers are usually treated more conservatively in low-bit CNN PTQ.
  • The method uses calibration data, but not the full training process required by QAT.

QAT Comparison

BRECQ is a PTQ method, but the paper compares it with QAT-style methods to show the cost/accuracy tradeoff.

The QAT comparison is the deployment argument. For ResNet-18 at 4/4, BRECQ reports 69.60 top-1 with 1024 calibration images and 0.4 GPU hours. QAT baselines use about 1.2M training images and roughly 100 GPU hours in this table.

For MobileNetV2, BRECQ reports 66.57 at 4/4 and 70.74 with mixed precision/8-bit activations. HAQ is still slightly higher in the mixed setting, but at a much higher training/search cost.

Mixed Precision

Mixed precision uses per-layer sensitivity to spend more bits where they matter most.

BRECQ also computes layer sensitivity and uses it for mixed precision. The curve is the useful part: under the same model-size or latency budget, mixed precision sits above uniform precision. That means the method is not only choosing better rounding; it is also learning where low-bit quantization hurts most.

This is the same lesson that later becomes common in deployment work: a single bit-width is easy to implement, but it is rarely the best accuracy-cost point.

Reading Notes

BRECQ’s main idea is modest but durable: choose the reconstruction unit that matches the model’s functional structure. For residual CNNs, that unit is usually a block.

The paper also separates two PTQ questions that are often mixed together:

  • How do we choose quantization parameters for a fixed bit-width?
  • How do we allocate bit-widths under a size or latency budget?

For LLM quantization, BRECQ is not a direct recipe, but it is useful background. GPTQ-style methods target different modules and use different solvers, yet they share the same philosophy: use calibration activations and sensitivity-aware reconstruction instead of minimizing a naive tensor error.
