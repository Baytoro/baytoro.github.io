LQ-Nets: Learned Quantization for Highly Accurate and Compact Deep Neural Networks

H.J. Chen
  2024-04-28 2024-04-28 Created  
  • Work hard 
  • >

  • Quantization 

Zhang D, Yang J, Ye D, Hua G. LQ-Nets: Learned Quantization for Highly Accurate and Compact Deep Neural Networks. arXiv:1807.10029.

Paper / Code

LQ-Nets is a useful paper to read before LSQ, PACT, and later learned data-type work because it asks a direct question: if the network weights and activations are not uniformly distributed, why should the quantization grid be fixed before training?

The answer in this paper is not just “learn a scale.” LQ-Nets learns the quantization basis itself. The quantization levels can become non-uniform, layer dependent, and channel dependent, while still keeping a decomposition that can be evaluated with bitwise dot products. That makes the paper more interesting than a plain learned-codebook method: the quantizer is flexible, but the authors still care about the low-bit inference path.

This note reads LQ-Nets as a quantization-aware training design:

  1. represent low-bit values through binary encodings and learned bases;
  2. update the quantizer in the forward pass with quantization error minimization;
  3. update the network weights with STE-based backpropagation;
  4. check whether the extra quantizer flexibility pays off at 1-4 bits.

All figures and tables below are cropped from the original paper PDF.

Motivation

Uniform quantization is attractive because it maps cleanly to integer arithmetic. But a fixed uniform grid is also a strong assumption. The distribution of a convolution filter can be close to symmetric and concentrated near zero; an activation distribution after BN/ReLU can be highly skewed and layer specific. A single handcrafted quantizer cannot be optimal for all of these cases.

Figure 1 shows this mismatch. The left two histograms are weights from different ResNet-20 layers. The right two histograms are activations. The shapes are different enough that “use the same uniform grid everywhere” is more of an engineering convenience than a statistically motivated choice.

The paper’s target is very low precision. At 8 bits, many uniform quantizers have enough levels to hide a mediocre grid. At 1-2 bits, every level matters. If a 2-bit quantizer gives only four reconstruction values, the exact placement of those four values can decide whether the model survives.

So LQ-Nets is hardware-aware, but not hardware-free. It preserves a bitwise decomposition, yet a real backend still needs kernels that exploit this decomposition efficiently.

Training Algorithm

The training setup keeps floating-point weights during optimization. Before convolution, weights and activations are quantized. After training, the floating-point weights can be discarded; the model keeps the binary encodings and quantizer bases.

The difficult part is learning the quantizer. A direct backpropagation update through a piecewise constant quantizer is weak because the derivative is zero almost everywhere. LQ-Nets therefore separates two jobs:

  • quantizer parameters are updated in the forward pass by QEM;
  • network parameters are updated in the backward pass with STE.

The paper uses a moving average factor of 0.9 and sets in the experiments. That sounds surprisingly small, but the basis from the previous training iteration is already a good warm start.

How It Is Used In The Network

The implementation choices matter because they define how much flexibility the quantizer actually has.

Component Paper setting
weight quantizer channel-wise, one quantizer per convolution filter
activation quantizer layer-wise, one quantizer per layer
first and last layers not quantized in the reported experiments
activation encoding in implementation
weight encoding in implementation
backward estimator STE
quantizer update QEM in forward pass

Channel-wise weight quantization is important. Filters in the same layer can still have different distributions. A single layer-wise basis for all filters would be cheaper, but it would remove much of the flexibility that the paper is testing.

Layer-wise activation quantization is a compromise. Per-channel activation quantization would be more flexible, but activations are dynamic and mini-batch sampled. The paper stabilizes activation bases with moving average updates.

QEM Versus Naive Backpropagation

The first result to read is not the large ImageNet table. It is the ablation that asks whether QEM is useful at all.

On ResNet-20/CIFAR-10, QEM beats direct BP for quantizer learning:

Bit-width W/A Quantizer optimization Accuracy
2/32 BP 90.0
2/32 QEM 91.8
2/2 BP 88.2
2/2 QEM 90.2

The improvement is not tiny. At 2-bit weights with full-precision activations, QEM gives +1.8 points. At 2-bit weights and 2-bit activations, it gives +2.0 points. The error curves in Figure 3 also show a cleaner gap through training, not just a lucky final checkpoint.

This is the paper’s strongest evidence that the quantizer should not merely be treated as another parameter passed through a crude STE. The assignment step and the least-squares basis update use the current tensor distribution more directly.

One QEM Iteration Is Enough

The next ablation checks how many alternating QEM updates are needed per training iteration.

The table reports ResNet-20/CIFAR-10 accuracy over five runs:

W/A T=1 T=2 T=3 T=4
1/2 88.37 88.38 88.45 88.51
2/2 90.16 90.35 90.33 90.25
3/3 91.58 91.55 91.62 91.44

The message is practical: extra inner iterations are not worth much. Because training itself repeats the update thousands of times, one QEM step per mini-batch is enough to track the moving tensor distribution. That matters for runtime. A quantizer that needs a heavy solver inside every forward pass would be difficult to justify.

Learned Quantizers Are Non-Uniform

The paper then checks the central claim: do the learned quantizers actually look different from uniform ones?

Figure 4 shows weights and activations before and after 2/2 quantization in a ResNet-20 trained on CIFAR-10. The orange diamonds mark the four quantization levels. The learned levels are visibly non-uniform, and they differ across layers.

My reading: this figure is more important than it first looks. It proves the method is not just learning a global scale hidden inside a different notation. The quantizer shape changes. For a 2-bit activation distribution that is concentrated near zero with a long tail, spending all four levels uniformly across the full range would be wasteful. LQ-Nets can put more resolution where the data actually sits.

That said, non-uniformity is not automatically a win. It is only useful if the backend can still execute the resulting representation efficiently. LQ-Nets earns the right to use non-uniform levels by generating them from bit-compatible bases.

ImageNet Sanity Check

Before the full comparison table, Table 3 compares 1/2 ResNet-18 ImageNet results against DoReFa-Net and HWGQ.

Method W/A Top-1 Top-5
Uniform DoReFa-Net 1/4 59.2 -
Half-wave Gaussian HWGQ 1/2 59.6 82.2
LQ-Nets 1/2 62.6 84.3

This is a clean comparison because HWGQ also targets 1-bit weights and 2-bit activations. LQ-Nets improves top-1 by 3.0 points and top-5 by 2.1 points under the same W/A bit-width. It also beats the DoReFa-Net 1/4 result while using fewer activation bits.

The reason I call this a sanity check is that CIFAR-10 results can hide a lot of quantization weaknesses. ImageNet with ResNet-18 is still small by modern standards, but it is much harder to pass with 1-2 bit tensors.

The table has two lessons.

First, weight-only quantization is much easier. ResNet-18 with 4-bit weights and full-precision activations reaches 70.0 top-1, only 0.3 below the full-precision 70.3 reported by the authors. This matches the broader quantization pattern: activations are often the harder part because they are input dependent and layer dependent.

Second, activation quantization needs a few bits on ImageNet. The jump from 1/2 to 3/3 on ResNet-18 is large: 62.6 to 68.2 top-1. The learned quantizer helps, but it does not make 1-bit weights and 2-bit activations free.

The careful reading is also important. These are not all apples-to-apples runtime comparisons. Some baselines use different full-precision references, some rows come from reported numbers in other papers, and first/last layers are not quantized. The accuracy trend is meaningful, but deployment speed still depends on the actual kernel implementation.

Training Cost

The paper also reports training overhead for ResNet-18. No bitwise operation is used in these training-time comparisons; this is about the cost of the quantization-aware training procedure itself.

W/A Training time
32/32 1.0x
2/32 1.4x
3/32 1.7x
1/2 2.1x
2/2 2.3x
3/3 3.7x

The cost grows with bit-width because the basis solve and the number of bit-plane interactions grow. The paper argues that solving is cheap and solving is small relative to convolution, but the measured overhead is still real. A 3/3 model taking 3.7x training time is not a free ablation.

This is one reason later practical QAT methods often prefer lower-dimensional quantizer parameters such as clipping thresholds or step sizes. They give up some codebook flexibility, but they are easier to train and deploy.

Relationship To PACT And LSQ

PACT, LSQ, and LQ-Nets all make the quantizer trainable, but they train different parts of it.

Method What is learned Grid shape Practical bias
PACT activation clipping threshold uniform after clipping simple activation QAT
LSQ step size uniform easy integer deployment
LQ-Nets basis-generated quantization levels non-uniform higher flexibility at low bits

PACT is mainly about learning how much activation range to keep. LSQ is about learning the step size for a uniform grid. LQ-Nets goes further by learning a basis that changes the relative spacing between levels.

That extra flexibility is exactly why LQ-Nets is a good conceptual baseline. It shows that “where the levels are” can matter as much as “how many levels exist.” But it also explains why LSQ-style methods became common baselines: uniform grids map more naturally to integer hardware and framework quantization APIs.

Reproduction Notes

If I were reproducing this paper, I would check these details first.

First, separate QEM from STE. QEM updates the quantizer in the forward pass; STE is only the surrogate gradient used for network training. Mixing these two concepts makes the implementation hard to debug.

Second, log the learned basis values per layer or per channel. If the levels remain close to a scaled uniform grid, the method is probably not exercising its main degree of freedom. Figure 4 gives a good qualitative target.

Third, reproduce the QEM-vs-BP ablation before chasing the full ImageNet table. Table 1 is a much smaller test of whether the quantizer optimizer is implemented correctly.

Fourth, keep the first and last layers consistent with the paper. Quantizing or not quantizing those layers can move low-bit accuracy enough to confuse comparisons.

Fifth, measure simulated accuracy and real backend speed separately. A PyTorch simulation can validate QAT, but it does not prove the bitwise inference path is fast. For deployment, the kernel needs to exploit bit packing, xnor, popcnt, and basis mixing.

Sixth, watch activation quantizer stability. Activation bases are estimated from mini-batches and then smoothed by moving average. A bad moving average schedule can make the quantizer chase batch noise.

Limitations

LQ-Nets is strong, but its scope is narrower than the title might suggest.

  • The experiments are CNN-focused: CIFAR-10 and ImageNet with AlexNet, VGG, GoogLeNet, ResNet, and DenseNet variants.
  • First and last layers remain full precision in the reported setup, which is common but still part of the accuracy story.
  • The inference format is bit-operation-compatible, but it is not a drop-in replacement for standard INT8 GEMM.
  • Training overhead is nontrivial, especially as both weight and activation bit-widths increase.
  • The paper predates modern deployment stacks, so the accuracy tables do not answer current backend questions such as TensorRT, mobile NPU support, compiler lowering, or mixed-precision fallback.

The main limitation for me is the backend gap. The learned basis is elegant, but a production system needs an efficient implementation of the full dot-product decomposition. Without that, “2-bit” can look better in a table than it behaves in an end-to-end latency benchmark.

Takeaway

LQ-Nets is the learned-codebook branch of early low-bit QAT, but with an important hardware constraint: the codebook is generated from binary bases so the dot product can still be decomposed into bitwise operations.

The paper’s strongest lessons are:

  • quantization levels should be treated as trainable model components;
  • non-uniform levels can recover accuracy at very low bit-widths;
  • QEM is a better quantizer update than naive backpropagation in this setting;
  • learned codebooks must be judged together with their kernel path.

When reading later quantization work, I keep LQ-Nets as a reference point: if a method learns only a scale, it is choosing deployability over codebook flexibility; if it learns arbitrary levels, it needs to explain the runtime path. LQ-Nets sits in the middle by learning non-uniform levels while preserving a bitwise-compatible decomposition.
