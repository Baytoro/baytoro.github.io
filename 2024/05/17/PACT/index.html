PACT: Parameterized Clipping Activation

H.J. Chen
  2024-05-17 2024-05-17 Created  
  • Paper Reading 
 
  • Quantization 

Paper: PACT: Parameterized Clipping Activation for Quantized Neural Networks

PACT is a quantization-aware training paper about one specific question: how should a network choose the clipping range for activations when the activation tensor only has a few bits?

That sounds like a calibration detail, but it is usually where low-bit activation quantization starts to break. If the clipping threshold is too low, the model throws away large feature values. If the threshold is too high, the quantizer spreads the same small number of levels across a wide range and common activations get coarse resolution. PACT turns that threshold into a learnable parameter, trains it with the task loss, and keeps the final inference path close to ordinary uniform quantization.

The paper is worth reading together with LSQ. PACT learns the activation clipping value ; LSQ later learns the quantization step size directly. Both papers make the same practical point: for QAT, the important quantization constants should not only be chosen by pre-training statistics.

Problem

Weight quantization is relatively stable because weights are fixed tensors after training. Their ranges can be inspected layer by layer, and many schemes can use per-channel scales or learned weight distributions. Activation quantization is less forgiving. Activations are input-dependent, layer-dependent, and often have a long tail because ReLU is unbounded.

The failure mode is visible in the paper’s first experiment.

Figure 1 compares ReLU, clipped activation, and quantized clipped activation on CIFAR10-ResNet20. Weight quantization alone is not the main source of damage here. The validation error jumps when activations are quantized without a good clipping policy. Clipping helps, but the clipping point cannot be arbitrary.

For a -bit unsigned activation quantizer, there are only representable codes. After ReLU, one code is naturally used for zero and the positive range is represented by a small grid. At 2 bits or 4 bits, the clipping threshold largely decides the model behavior:

Threshold choice Main effect Failure mode
Small Fine resolution near zero Useful large activations saturate
Large Less saturation Ordinary values suffer large rounding error
Learned Task loss searches the tradeoff Requires QAT and regularization

PACT is built around this tradeoff.

The point is not simply to make small. The point is to prevent the learned activation from drifting back toward an almost unbounded ReLU. Without that pressure, a large can reduce clipping error while making low-bit rounding error worse.

Figure 2 shows the learned values for different layers of CIFAR10-ResNet20. They start high and then settle to layer-specific values. This already argues against a single global clipping constant. Early, middle, and late activations do not need the same dynamic range.

Two implementation details matter here:

  1. The paper uses one per activation layer in its main setting.
  2. It finds that setting to the same coefficient used for weight L2 regularization works well in practice.

That is a fairly simple recipe. It is also hardware-friendly because the inference graph only needs a clipped activation scale, not a learned non-uniform codebook.

The important reading is that “good clipping” is not a property of the tensor distribution alone. It is a property of the quantized network under task loss.

Experimental Setup

PACT is evaluated as a QAT method for CNNs. The paper tests:

Dataset or model Network
CIFAR10 ResNet20
SVHN ResNet20
ImageNet AlexNet
ImageNet ResNet18
ImageNet ResNet50

The paper quantizes activations with PACT and weights with DoReFa-style weight quantization. The main experiments follow the common convention of keeping the first and last layers unquantized, because these layers are unusually sensitive. The appendix later studies what happens when those layers are quantized.

This setup matters because PACT is not a post-training quantization method. It assumes the network is trained, or at least fine-tuned, while the quantization operators are present in the forward pass.

Accuracy Results

The main result is that learnable activation clipping makes very low activation precision much less brittle.

Figure 4 shows training and validation curves across CIFAR10, SVHN, AlexNet, ResNet18, and ResNet50. The high-level pattern is consistent: as activation precision increases, PACT approaches the full-precision baseline. The paper reports that validation error is within about 1 percentage point of full precision once activations use at least 4 bits.

The degradation plot in Figure 4 is also important. On ImageNet ResNet18 and ResNet50, PACT has lower top-1 accuracy degradation than the compared activation quantization schemes at the same activation bitwidths.

Figure 5 compares PACT with prior quantization schemes on AlexNet, ResNet18, and ResNet50. The exact experimental settings differ across earlier papers, so this should not be read as a perfectly controlled ablation. Still, it shows the practical motivation: activation clipping was one of the missing pieces for making 4-bit activations usable on larger CNNs.

The paper also provides a direct DoReFa versus PACT table.

The ResNet50 line is the clearest. DoReFa-style activation quantization at 4 bits reaches 0.714 top-1 accuracy, while PACT reaches 0.765, close to the 0.769 full-precision reference used in the table. At 5 bits, PACT reaches 0.917 on CIFAR10, 0.979 on SVHN, 0.557 on AlexNet, 0.698 on ResNet18, and 0.767 on ResNet50. The method is not only saving memory; it is making the low-bit activation path trainable enough that the model can recover most of the lost accuracy.

System Motivation

PACT is a training method, but the paper also includes a hardware analysis to explain why sub-8-bit activations are worth the trouble.

Figure 6 studies a precision-configurable MAC design and a ResNet50 inference system model. The main observation is that reducing bit precision can improve both compute density and memory behavior. The paper reports about 14x MAC density improvement when moving from 16-bit to 2-bit precision.

The system-level result is not limited to peak FLOPs. Under a 64 GBps memory bandwidth setting, moving from 16 bits to 4 bits gives a 4x peak FLOP increase but a 4.5x performance improvement. The extra gain comes from memory behavior: lower precision lets the same on-chip memory hold more data and reduces off-chip traffic pressure.

The paper keeps first and last layers at 8 bits for the 4-bit and 2-bit configurations in this analysis. It estimates that quantizing those layers too could add another 1.24x performance improvement, which motivates the appendix experiments on first and last layer sensitivity.

Ablation: Scope Of Alpha

PACT needs to decide how many clipping parameters to learn. The paper compares three choices:

Scope Meaning
Per channel Different for each activation channel
Per layer One for each activation layer
Per network One global shared by the network

Figure 7 shows that per-layer and per-channel clipping behave similarly, while per-network clipping is worse. The paper chooses per-layer clipping because it is simpler for hardware. With one activation scale per layer, the scaling factor can be applied after accumulation rather than changing inside the inner MAC loop.

This is a useful engineering compromise. Per-channel clipping might be slightly more expressive, but activation per-channel scaling is more intrusive than per-channel weight scaling. PACT chooses the simpler runtime contract.

Ablation: Alpha Regularization

The regularizer on is not optional in spirit. Without it, the learned threshold can stay too large and the low-bit quantizer behaves too much like a naive ReLU quantizer.

Figure 8 compares different values on CIFAR10-ResNet20. The paper’s practical recommendation is simple: use L2 regularization on and set its coefficient to the same value as the weight L2 coefficient. That makes PACT relatively easy to add to a normal training recipe.

The plot also shows why this is not just “make alpha small.” Too much regularization can damage validation error because it forces excessive clipping. The useful range is the range where is small enough to give a dense quantization grid but large enough not to erase important activations.

First And Last Layers

The main experiments keep first and last layers in full precision. The paper then checks how risky that assumption is.

Table 2 reports CIFAR10-ResNet20 validation error when the middle layers use 2 to 5 bits and the first and last layers use different precisions. The pattern is clear: quantizing first and last layers to 8 bits causes little damage, but using 2 or 4 bits there is much more dangerous.

Figure 9 gives the same message from training curves. Quantizing only the first layer or only the last layer already hurts. Quantizing both hurts more. This is why many practical quantization papers leave those layers at a higher precision even when most of the network is aggressively quantized.

For PACT, this means the headline “4-bit activations” should be read carefully. The middle layers can use low precision, but deployment recipes often keep the boundary layers special.

Implementation Notes

A minimal PACT module only needs three pieces:

  1. A learnable positive clipping parameter.
  2. A clipped activation function.
  3. A fake-quantized forward path during training.

  • Use an STE or framework fake-quant operator so gradients through rounding are defined as intended.
  • Keep positive. The paper initializes it high and regularizes it; implementations often clamp it or parameterize it through a positive transform.
  • Add to the training loss.
  • Decide explicitly whether first and last layers are left full precision, quantized to 8 bits, or included in the same low-bit policy.

At inference time, PACT does not require a complicated activation function. The learned gives the activation scale . The activation tensor is clipped, quantized to an unsigned integer range, and consumed by the low-precision layer.

Relation To Later Quantization Work

PACT is a CNN-era QAT method, but the idea keeps reappearing.

LSQ learns the step size instead of directly learning the clipping bound. For unsigned activations, these are closely related because . LSQ also adds a careful gradient scaling rule for the learned step size, which PACT does not emphasize.

Post-training LLM quantization methods attack a harder version of the same activation range problem. LLM.int8() isolates activation outliers, SmoothQuant migrates activation magnitude into weights, and OmniQuant learns clipping and equivalent transformations during calibration. Those methods are different because full QAT is usually too expensive for large language models, but the pressure is the same: activation ranges cannot be treated as passive min/max statistics.

PACT is therefore best read as a clean early example of learnable activation range control. It is not the direct recipe for a 70B LLM, but it is one of the simplest papers showing why the activation range belongs inside the optimization problem.

Limitations

PACT has several important limits:

  • It requires quantization-aware training. That is fine for CIFAR or ImageNet CNNs, but expensive for large pretrained models.
  • The paper mostly uses one activation clipping value per layer. That is simple and hardware-friendly, but less expressive than per-channel, per-token, or group-wise activation control.
  • The method needs an extra regularization coefficient for , even if the paper finds a simple default.
  • The strongest results rely on keeping first and last layers unquantized or at higher precision.
  • The experiments are from the CNN quantization setting. Transformers and LLMs have different activation outlier structure and often need PTQ-friendly methods.

These limitations do not weaken the main contribution. They define where the contribution applies.

Takeaway

PACT turns activation clipping from a fixed calibration choice into a trained model parameter. That is the whole paper, and it is enough.
